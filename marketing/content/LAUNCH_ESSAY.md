# Substack launch essay (draft)

## Title options (4 U's)
1. A new map of everything we know, built for the age of AI
2. Every sentence has an owner. Here is the map.
3. I classified all of human knowledge, starting from mathematics

## Draft
Machines now write fluent text about anything, in seconds. They cannot tell you which discipline is qualified to judge a sentence, or what evidence would settle it. Most of our classification systems cannot either, because they were built to put books on shelves.

So I built a new one. It is called Taxonomy.

It starts where all exact knowledge starts, with mathematics. All 63 top-level classes of the Mathematics Subject Classification sit in five cells, ordered by what a proof may assume. From there the same discipline extends to 24 more fields: physics and law, medicine and history, strategy and religion, and Indigenous knowledge, which is held back until Māori-led review.

Every claim gets three things:
- **An owner:** the field whose standard of evidence can establish it.
- **A cell:** the part of that field it concerns.
- **A warrant:** the kind of evidence it owes, from proof to a court's finding.

Take one tax. "Consumers bear most of it" is economics. "The importer must pay it" is law. "Our March return shows $42,000" is accounting. Three sentences, three owners, three kinds of proof.

Why it matters now:
- An AI answer can be checked claim by claim against the evidence each claim needs.
- A policy brief can show which parts are measured and which are choices.
- A designer can see a product from every field at once. I did this for a pair of AI-enabled sunglasses.

You can try it today: [link]. Type any sentence, or any subject, and read what 25 fields say about it.

Tell me where it is wrong. That is how a standard gets good.
