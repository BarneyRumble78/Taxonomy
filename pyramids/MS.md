## 4. Face O — Objects

### MS.O.A1 Mental processes

Situation. Psychology's core is the process that turns input into report and act. Experimental psychology, psychophysics and cognitive science work at this unit, mostly with human participants in controlled tasks. Complication. Its laws hold over stated ranges, and its biases are relative to a stated norm. A change of range or norm changes the verdict. Most samples come from industrialised, educated populations (Henrich et al. 2010). Question. Which processes does a claim about the working mind concern? Answer. Five, ordered from input to evaluation.

Key line (inductive; plural noun processes; dimension stage of the processing; order input to evaluation). Members (5): sensation and perception · attention and consciousness · learning and memory · emotion and motivation · thought, language and judgement.

- **MS.O.A1.1 Sensation and perception.** **A stimulus is transduced; a percept is constructed.** Named objects:
  - MS.O.A1.1.1 Weber's law: over a mid-range of intensities, the just-noticeable difference ΔI is a roughly constant fraction k of the base intensity I (Gescheider 1997).
  - MS.O.A1.1.2 Fechner's law: if Weber's law holds and each JND is a unit step, sensation grows with the logarithm of intensity.
  - MS.O.A1.1.3 Stevens's power law: for direct magnitude estimation over a stated range, perceived magnitude is a power function of intensity.
  - MS.O.A1.1.4 Signal detection theory: under equal-variance Gaussian noise and signal distributions, d′ measures sensitivity and c (or β) measures criterion. A classical threshold confounds the two (Green and Swets 1966).
- **MS.O.A1.2 Attention and consciousness.** **Selection has a cost; reportable experience has measurable conditions.**
  - MS.O.A1.2.1 Selective attention and capacity limits; inattentional blindness.
  - MS.O.A1.2.2 Consciousness as reportable experience, studied with no-report controls. The hard problem is PL.O.A2; MS uses it and does not claim to solve it.
  - MS.O.A1.2.3 Hypnosis and altered states, as changes in attention and report (APA Div. 30 home).
- **MS.O.A1.3 Learning and memory.** **Experience changes later behaviour, and what is retained depends on how it was encoded and retrieved.**
  - MS.O.A1.3.1 Classical conditioning: a cue comes to predict an outcome. Rescorla–Wagner model: on each trial, ΔV = αβ(λ − ΣV), where α and β are cue and outcome salience rates in (0,1], λ is the outcome's maximum, and ΣV sums all cues present.
  - MS.O.A1.3.2 Operant conditioning and schedules of reinforcement.
  - MS.O.A1.3.3 Memory systems: declarative (episodic, semantic) and nondeclarative (procedural, priming, classical conditioning, nonassociative) (Squire 2004).
  - MS.O.A1.3.4 Working-memory capacity: about 3–5 chunks when rehearsal and chunking are blocked (Cowan 2001). The 7±2 figure is a heuristic (register).
  - MS.O.A1.3.5 Forgetting. Cue-dependent retrieval failure (Tulving 1974) is one answer. Interference, decay and failed consolidation are rivals (CONTESTED; Wixted 2004).
- **MS.O.A1.4 Emotion and motivation.** **A state has a trigger, a bodily change and an action tendency.**
  - MS.O.A1.4.1 Appraisal accounts and basic-emotion accounts, as rival answer-families.
  - MS.O.A1.4.2 Motivation and reward as process claims. The tissue (orbitofrontal cortex, vmPFC, ventral striatum) is MS.O.A2.3.
- **MS.O.A1.5 Thought, language and judgement.** **Thought is shaped by categories, language and shortcuts, and its errors can be scored against a stated norm.**
  - MS.O.A1.5.1 Concepts and categories.
  - MS.O.A1.5.2 Judgement heuristics (availability, representativeness, anchoring; Tversky and Kahneman 1974). They are biases relative to a stated normative model. The rule of thumb used by an analyst is a different sentence, in the register.
  - MS.O.A1.5.3 Psycholinguistic processing (comprehension, production). The grammar itself is LN.
  - MS.O.A1.5.4 Creativity and problem solving (APA Div. 10 home). The aesthetic verdict is AR.
  - MS.O.A1.5.5 RETIRED in Phase 2.1 (ID not reused). Learning claims under an educational arrangement, including physics and mathematics education (MSC 97), are ED.O.A2–A4. MS keeps the general mechanism of learning and memory in MS.O.A1.5.

Named objects: Weber's law (MS.O.A1.1.1; mid-range intensities, the just-noticeable difference a constant fraction of base intensity). Fechner's law (MS.O.A1.1.2; assumes Weber's law and equal steps per just-noticeable difference). Stevens's power law (MS.O.A1.1.3; direct magnitude estimation over a stated range). Signal detection theory (MS.O.A1.1.4; equal-variance Gaussian evidence for the d′ index). Rescorla–Wagner model (MS.O.A1.3.1; learning driven by prediction error, cues present together share one error term). Memory-systems taxonomy (MS.O.A1.3.3; Squire 2004). Cowan's capacity limit (MS.O.A1.3.4; rehearsal and chunking blocked). Availability, representativeness and anchoring heuristics (MS.O.A1.5.2; biases against a stated norm). The drift-diffusion model is used from MS.M.4 (two-choice, fast single-stage decisions; drift constant within a trial).

### MS.O.A2 Nervous systems

Situation. Neuroscience's core runs from molecule to system. It records from, perturbs and models nervous tissue, in humans and in other animals. Complication. v1 filed lesion, imaging and computational models here, which are methods. Inferring a process from a region's activation is also unsafe, because most regions serve more than one function (Poldrack 2006). Question. Which tissue claims does neuroscience own? Answer. Four, ordered by scale and then by time.

Key line (inductive; plural noun levels; dimension scale of the tissue, then time; order cell to system, then development). Members (4): neurons, glia and synapses · plasticity · circuits and systems · development of the nervous system.

- **MS.O.A2.1 Neurons, glia and synapses.** **Nerve cells signal by spikes and by synaptic transmission, and glia take part in that signalling.**
  - MS.O.A2.1.1 Action potential: an all-or-none spike, given a threshold depolarisation of an excitable membrane.
  - MS.O.A2.1.2 Chemical and electrical synapses; excitation and inhibition.
  - MS.O.A2.1.3 Glia: astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, microglia and their signalling roles (SfN Theme B).
  - The equilibrium potential (Nernst, GHK) uses CH.O.A3.3. The cell as a cell is BI; electrical signalling is native here.
- **MS.O.A2.2 Plasticity.** **Activity changes synaptic strength, and the change can persist or be regulated.** Key line (inductive; plural noun forms of change; dimension the rule that governs the change; order from the effect to the rules that shape it). Members (3): long-term synaptic change · timing-dependent rule · homeostatic regulation.
  - MS.O.A2.2.1 Long-term potentiation and depression, as candidate mechanisms of learning. A changed synapse is not yet a memory; the memory is MS.O.A1.3.
  - MS.O.A2.2.2 **Hebbian rule and spike-timing dependence.** A synapse strengthens when presynaptic activity helps to fire the postsynaptic cell (Hebb 1949). In cultured hippocampal neurons, a presynaptic spike that precedes a postsynaptic spike by tens of milliseconds potentiates the synapse, and the reverse order depresses it (Bi and Poo 1998). Hypothesis: the timing window is preparation-specific and has not been shown at every synapse type (CONTESTED as a general rule).
  - MS.O.A2.2.3 **Homeostatic regulation.** After a sustained change in activity, neurons scale their synaptic strengths in the opposite direction, which keeps firing within a range (Turrigiano et al. 1998). Hypothesis: the scaling is multiplicative across a neuron's synapses, and it acts over hours to days. It limits runaway feedback from the Hebbian rule in MS.O.A2.2.2.
- **MS.O.A2.3 Circuits and systems.** **A function is carried by a pathway or circuit, and a test of function needs both lesion or stimulation and recording.**
  - MS.O.A2.3.1 Sensory and motor pathways.
  - MS.O.A2.3.2 Hypothalamus and homeostasis.
  - MS.O.A2.3.3 Valuation circuits: orbitofrontal cortex, vmPFC, ventral striatum. "Limbic system" is a contested grouping and is not used as a cell.
  - MS.O.A2.3.4 Localisation as a starting map; a function is usually a circuit.
- **MS.O.A2.4 Development of the nervous system.** **The nervous system is built by overproduction followed by experience-dependent pruning, within windows of sensitivity.**
  - MS.O.A2.4.1 Overproduction and pruning.
  - MS.O.A2.4.2 Critical and sensitive periods, with their measured windows.

Named objects: Action potential (MS.O.A2.1.1; threshold depolarisation of an excitable membrane). Hodgkin–Huxley model (MS.O.A2.1.1; a uniform membrane patch, ion channels as independent gates, parameters fitted to the cell type). Cable equation (MS.O.A2.1.1; passive membrane, uniform cylinder, current flowing along the axis). Long-term potentiation and depression (MS.O.A2.2.1; the induction protocol is stated). Hebbian rule and spike-timing-dependent plasticity (MS.O.A2.2.2). Synaptic scaling (MS.O.A2.2.3). Integrate-and-fire network model (MS.O.A2.3; spike shape ignored, each cell a single compartment). Temporal-difference learning model (MS.O.A2.3.3; learning driven by the gap between expected and received reward). Critical-period windows (MS.O.A2.4.2). Fitting of these models is a practice in MS.M.4.

### MS.O.A3 Persons

Situation. Persons differ stably and change across the life course. Personality, developmental, clinical and differential psychology describe both. Complication. Differences invite reification, and clinical categories are conventions. A heritable difference is not a fixed one, and a score is not a thing. Question. Which claims concern the person as the unit? Answer. Four, ordered from stable difference to disorder.

Key line (inductive; plural noun aspects; dimension what persists or changes in the person; order from stable trait to clinical pattern). Members (4): personality · intelligence and abilities · life-course development · psychopathology.

- **MS.O.A3.1 Personality.** **Stable differences in thought, feeling and behaviour can be summarised by a small number of dimensions, and the situation also matters.**
  - MS.O.A3.1.1 Five-factor model as the common answer. HEXACO (six factors; Ashton and Lee 2007) is the rival (CONTESTED).
  - MS.O.A3.1.2 Person–situation debate: both variances are measured.
- **MS.O.A3.2 Intelligence and abilities.** **Scores on varied ability tests correlate positively, and part of the variance in them is associated with inherited differences in a stated population.**
  - MS.O.A3.2.1 g as a statistical factor (Spearman 1904), not a thing seen.
  - MS.O.A3.2.2 Heritability of intelligence: a variance fraction in a stated population, environment and time, broad (H²) or narrow (h²). The quantitative-genetics result uses BI.O.A4.
- **MS.O.A3.3 Life-course development.** **Persons change in patterned ways across the life course, and the patterns are ranges more than fixed stages.**
  - MS.O.A3.3.1 Stage theories as ideal types.
  - MS.O.A3.3.2 Attachment classification (Ainsworth; Main and Solomon).
  - MS.O.A3.3.3 Ageing and adult development.
- **MS.O.A3.4 Psychopathology.** **The psychological process account of distress and disability.**
  - MS.O.A3.4.1 Diagnostic categories as conventions: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022) and ICD-11 (WHO, in force 2022). The category is W12.
  - MS.O.A3.4.2 RDoC as the process-first alternative (CONTESTED as a basis for classification).
  - Disorder as function failure, its treatment and its trials are MD.O.A1. MS.O.A3.4 holds the process account only (CONTESTED; alternative: MD owns both).

Named objects: Five-factor model (MS.O.A3.1.1; trait terms sampled from language, factor structure replicated in the sample studied, invariance across cultures not assumed). HEXACO model (MS.O.A3.1.1; adds honesty–humility as a sixth factor, same hypotheses; CONTESTED). g (MS.O.A3.2.1; a positive manifold across a test battery, read as a statistical factor). Heritability of intelligence (MS.O.A3.2.2; a variance fraction for a stated population and environment; uses BI.O.A4). Attachment classification (MS.O.A3.3.2; the Strange Situation procedure with infant and caregiver in a stated age range). DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 categories (MS.O.A3.4.1; conventions, W12). RDoC (MS.O.A3.4.2; a process-first matrix, OPEN as a basis for classification). The twin design, factor analysis and item response theory are used from MS.M.1 (twin design: equal environments for the two twin types, no assortative mating, estimates apply to the population studied).

### MS.O.A4 Interactions

Situation. Something passes between persons: pressure, information, attitude, norm and recognition. Social psychology studies it by experiment and by coded observation. Complication. Classic effects have mixed replication records. A collaborative project replicated fewer than half of 100 published findings by the significance criterion (Open Science Collaboration 2015). Samples are narrow (Henrich et al. 2010). Question. Which claims concern what passes between persons? Answer. Five, ordered from the situation to the self it builds.

Key line (inductive; plural noun interactions; dimension what passes between persons; order from situation to self). Members (5): social influence · social cognition · attitudes · groups and relationships · self and identity.

- **MS.O.A4.1 Social influence.** **The presence, majority view or instruction of others changes what a person does, by amounts that depend on the situation.** Key line (inductive; plural noun pressures; dimension the source of the pressure; order from peers to authority to onlookers). Members (3): conformity · obedience · bystander response. Conformity, obedience and bystander effects, each with its replication record (Burger 2009; Fischer et al. 2011 meta-analysis on bystanders). Environment–behaviour claims (APA Div. 34) sit here.
  - MS.O.A4.1.1 **Conformity to a unanimous majority.** In line-judgement tasks, participants facing a unanimous majority of confederates gave the majority's wrong answer on a substantial share of critical trials, and one dissenting confederate lowered the rate (Asch 1951, 1956). The pooled size is UNVERIFIED here. Rates vary by culture and period (Bond and Smith 1996). Hypothesis: the correct answer is unambiguous, the judgement is public and the majority is unanimous.
  - MS.O.A4.1.2 **Obedience to an experimenter.** Under Milgram's standard instructions most participants continued to the maximum shock, and continuation fell when the experimenter was absent or the learner was near (Milgram 1963, 1974). A partial replication that stopped at 150 volts found a comparable continuation rate (Burger 2009). Whether participants obeyed or identified with the scientific aim is CONTESTED.
  - MS.O.A4.1.3 **Bystander response.** The perceived presence of other bystanders lowers the chance that a given person helps in an emergency (Darley and Latané 1968). The inhibition is smaller when the emergency is clearly dangerous (Fischer et al. 2011; the size of this moderator is UNVERIFIED here). Hypothesis: the bystanders cannot easily see or signal one another.
- **MS.O.A4.2 Social cognition.** **People explain and predict others' acts by inferring causes, categories and traits, and the inferences are systematically patterned.** Key line (inductive; plural noun inferences; dimension what is inferred about the other person; order from cause to role to category). Members (3): correspondence of act and disposition · actor–observer difference · implicit category association. Attribution; the fundamental attribution error (Ross 1977), flagged as weaker in non-WEIRD samples (Henrich et al. 2010).
  - MS.O.A4.2.1 **Correspondence bias.** Observers infer an author's attitude from an essay even when told that the essay's position was assigned (Jones and Harris 1967). This is the experimental form of the fundamental attribution error. Hypothesis: the observer knows the assignment, and the essay is the only information about the author.
  - MS.O.A4.2.2 **Actor–observer asymmetry.** Actors are said to explain their own acts by the situation, and observers to explain the same acts by disposition (Jones and Nisbett 1971). A meta-analysis found the asymmetry weak or absent as stated (Malle 2006), so the claim is CONTESTED.
  - MS.O.A4.2.3 **Implicit association measures.** Response-time differences in the Implicit Association Test index associations between categories and attributes (Greenwald et al. 1998). Their prediction of single behaviours is small, and what the test measures is CONTESTED. Hypothesis: block order and category pairing are counterbalanced.
- **MS.O.A4.3 Attitudes.** **An attitude is a learned evaluation that can change after the person acts, and that predicts action under stated conditions.** Key line (inductive; plural noun links; dimension the direction between attitude and act; order from act to attitude, attitude to act, then message to attitude). Members (3): attitude change after action · attitude–behaviour correspondence · persuasion by message. Cognitive dissonance (Festinger 1957), with self-perception as the rival account. Consumer attitudes (APA Div. 23) sit here; BU.O.A4.1 uses them.
  - MS.O.A4.3.1 **Insufficient justification.** Participants paid one dollar to tell another person that a dull task was enjoyable later rated the task more enjoyable than participants paid twenty dollars (Festinger and Carlsmith 1959). Self-perception theory predicts the same pattern by a different route (Bem 1967). Hypothesis (dissonance): the act is freely chosen and its external justification is weak.
  - MS.O.A4.3.2 **Attitude–behaviour correspondence.** A general attitude predicts a single act poorly, and an attitude measured at the same specificity as the behaviour predicts it better (Ajzen and Fishbein 1977). The theory of planned behaviour adds subjective norm and perceived control as predictors of intention (Ajzen 1991). Hypothesis: the behaviour is under volitional control, and the predictors share its target, action, context and time.
  - MS.O.A4.3.3 **Dual-process persuasion.** Argument quality shapes attitude when people are motivated and able to think, and cues such as source status shape it otherwise (Petty and Cacioppo 1986; elaboration likelihood model). A single-route account is the rival (Kruglanski and Thompson 1999; CONTESTED).
- **MS.O.A4.4 Groups and relationships.** **Behaviour in a group differs from the same person's behaviour alone, and the difference depends on identifiability, contact and shared timing.** Key line (inductive; plural noun settings; dimension the number and kind of interacting parties; order from many actors to two). Members (3): small-group effort · intergroup contact · conversational timing. Small groups, roles, norms, status; couples and families; conversation as turn-taking; human–animal interaction (CONTESTED; alternative BI.O.A3).
  - MS.O.A4.4.1 **Social loafing.** Individual effort on additive tasks falls when contributions are pooled and cannot be identified (Latané, Williams and Harkins 1979; meta-analysis by Karau and Williams 1993). Hypothesis: output is measured per person, and identifiability is manipulated.
  - MS.O.A4.4.2 **Intergroup contact.** Contact between members of two groups lowers prejudice when it involves equal status, common goals, cooperation and institutional support (Allport 1954). A meta-analysis found lower prejudice after contact, and found these conditions helpful but not necessary (Pettigrew and Tropp 2006). Selection of contact by prior attitude is the main defeater.
  - MS.O.A4.4.3 **Conversational timing.** Gaps between turns in informal conversation cluster near 200 milliseconds across ten languages, with differences in the mean gap between languages (Stivers et al. 2009). Hypothesis: recordings are coded for turn boundaries with inter-coder reliability stated.
- **MS.O.A4.5 Self and identity.** **The self is built from others' appraisals, claimed categories and the body, and each source can be manipulated.** Key line (inductive; plural noun sources of the self; dimension where the self-description comes from; order from others' appraisal to category to body). Members (3): appraisal by others · group category · bodily self. The self as a role taken toward others; identity as a claimed category; body and embodiment. The category's distribution is MS.O.A5.1.
  - MS.O.A4.5.1 **Looking-glass self.** The self-concept is built partly from the appraisals a person believes others hold (Cooley 1902). Beliefs about how others see one track the general view of the person more than any single observer's view (Kenny and DePaulo 1993; UNVERIFIED in detail). Hypothesis: others' appraisals are measured apart from the self-ratings.
  - MS.O.A4.5.2 **Minimal group effect.** Assigning people to arbitrary categories is enough to produce in-group favouritism in allocation tasks (Tajfel et al. 1971). Social identity theory explains it by a need for positive group distinctiveness (Tajfel and Turner 1979), and that explanation is CONTESTED. Hypothesis: no prior contact, anonymity and no material interest in the category.
  - MS.O.A4.5.3 **Bodily self.** Synchronous touch to a hidden hand and a visible rubber hand produces felt ownership of the rubber hand (Botvinick and Cohen 1998). Hypothesis: the touches are synchronous within a short delay, and the rubber hand lies in a plausible position. The neural mechanism of body ownership is MS.O.A2.3.

Named objects: Asch line-judgement paradigm (MS.O.A4.1.1; unambiguous stimulus, public answer, unanimous majority). Milgram obedience paradigm (MS.O.A4.1.2). Bystander effect (MS.O.A4.1.3; bystanders unable to see or signal one another). Correspondence bias and the fundamental attribution error (MS.O.A4.2.1; assigned position known to the observer). Implicit Association Test (MS.O.A4.2.3; counterbalanced blocks, response-time contrast). Cognitive dissonance theory (MS.O.A4.3.1; a freely chosen act with weak external justification). Self-perception theory (MS.O.A4.3.1; the rival account). Theory of planned behaviour (MS.O.A4.3.2; volitional behaviour, predictors matched in specificity). Elaboration likelihood model (MS.O.A4.3.3; CONTESTED). Social-loafing effect (MS.O.A4.4.1). Contact hypothesis (MS.O.A4.4.2). Minimal-group paradigm and social identity theory (MS.O.A4.5.2; the explanation is CONTESTED). Rubber-hand illusion (MS.O.A4.5.3).

### MS.O.A5 Social structures

Situation. Sociology's core is the pattern that outlasts its members. Sociology, anthropology, demography and criminology describe it from registers, surveys, records and field accounts. Complication. v1 mixed movement into population and sector into institution. A pattern in aggregates also does not transfer to individuals without a further argument, which is the ecological fallacy. Question. Which claims concern a pattern no single person carries? Answer. Five, ordered from position to change.

Key line (inductive; plural noun structures; dimension the kind of pattern; order from position to change). Members (5): positions and inequality · organisations · institutions and culture · populations · collective action and change.

- **MS.O.A5.1 Positions and inequality.** **Positions carry unequal life chances, and the size of a gap depends on the controls and the measure chosen.** Key line (inductive; plural noun comparisons; dimension what is compared across positions; order from persons to groups to the whole distribution). Members (3): status attainment · residual group gaps · distribution and mobility. Status, role, role conflict. Inequality as a residual in life chances after stated controls. Bases include class, race, gender, sexuality and disability.
  - MS.O.A5.1.1 **Status attainment.** In a 1962 sample of US men, father's occupation and education were related to the son's occupational status, and the son's own education carried much of that relation (Blau and Duncan 1967). Hypothesis: a linear path model, men in the labour force, one country and one period. Whether it holds for women and other periods is an open test.
  - MS.O.A5.1.2 **Residual group gaps.** The part of a wage gap between two groups left after controls for measured characteristics is estimated by the Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition (Blinder 1973; Oaxaca 1973; uses MA.O.A5 for the regression). Hypothesis: linear wage equations for each group, measured characteristics and a stated reference set of coefficients. The residual is not a measure of discrimination, because omitted variables and bad controls remain.
  - MS.O.A5.1.3 **Distribution and mobility.** The Gini coefficient summarises inequality from the Lorenz curve, with the income concept and the unit stated. An intergenerational mobility table compares parents' and children's positions. Hypothesis: positions are measured comparably across generations. The two summaries answer different questions, so a change in one does not imply a change in the other.
- **MS.O.A5.2 Organisations.** **An organisation is a structure of rules, positions and ties that persists as members change, and its parts vary separately.** Key line (inductive; plural noun structures; dimension how the pattern is held in place; order from formal rule to informal tie to the field). Members (3): formal structure · informal ties · organisational fields. Weberian bureaucracy (rule, file, career); informal structure. PO.O.A2 uses it for state bureaucracy.
  - MS.O.A5.2.1 **Dimensions of formal structure.** Bureaucracy is an ideal type of rule, hierarchy, file and career (Weber). Empirical studies measured formalisation, specialisation and centralisation as separate dimensions that need not rise together (Aston studies; UNVERIFIED here). Hypothesis: comparable measures for a set of organisations at one level.
  - MS.O.A5.2.2 **Weak ties.** In a sample of professional workers, jobs were found more often through acquaintances than through close contacts (Granovetter 1973, 1974). Hypothesis: a bridging tie links otherwise separate clusters. Whether weak ties generally carry more useful information is CONTESTED.
  - MS.O.A5.2.3 **Institutional isomorphism.** Organisations in a shared field become more alike through coercive, mimetic and normative pressures (DiMaggio and Powell 1983). Hypothesis: the field is defined in advance and similarity is measured at more than one date. Functionalist circularity is the usual defeater.
- **MS.O.A5.3 Institutions and culture.** **Repeated patterns and shared meanings differ systematically between groups and periods, and their rates can be compared.** Key line (inductive; plural noun patterns; dimension what is shared or repeated; order from practice to taste to rate). Members (3): religious practice and belonging · taste and position · social integration and rates. An institution is a repeated, taken-for-granted pattern: family, market, state, religion, school, media, science. Culture is shared meaning in use. Consumption as practice sits here. A paper merely set in a church or school is a sector instance.
  - MS.O.A5.3.1 **Secularisation.** In many high-income countries, rates of religious attendance fell over the twentieth century, and the pace differed by country. Whether modernisation causes the fall is CONTESTED. The claim concerns practice and belonging, so W13 does not apply. Hypothesis: attendance and belonging are measured comparably across surveys and years.
  - MS.O.A5.3.2 **Taste and position.** Tastes in music, food and art differ by education and class position, and they mark status (Bourdieu 1984). The omnivore thesis holds that high-status groups now mix highbrow and popular tastes (Peterson and Kern 1996). Which account fits is CONTESTED. Hypothesis: tastes are measured by survey in one country and period, with education as the position measure.
  - MS.O.A5.3.3 **Social integration and rates.** Suicide rates differed by religion and marital status in nineteenth-century European registers, and Durkheim explained the pattern by social integration (Durkheim 1897). Undercount and changes of definition are the main defeaters. The clinical account is MD.O.A1 (uses MD.O.A1). An aggregate rate alone does not license a claim about individuals.
- **MS.O.A5.4 Populations.** **A human population changes by births, deaths and migration, and its size and age make-up follow from those rates.** Key line (inductive; plural noun demographic steps; dimension the event or operation on the population; order from birth to death to movement to projection). Members (4): fertility · mortality · migration · projection. Births, deaths, migration and their rates; life table; cohort. Human population is owned here; non-human population dynamics are BI.O.A5.
  - MS.O.A5.4.1 **Fertility measures.** The total fertility rate sums age-specific birth rates for one period. Hypothesis: current age-specific rates hold through a woman's life. When the age at birth shifts, the period rate differs from completed cohort fertility (Bongaarts and Feeney 1998).
  - MS.O.A5.4.2 **Survival measures.** A life table turns age-specific death rates into survival and life expectancy. Hypothesis: a period table assumes today's rates hold through a lifetime. Adult mortality rises roughly exponentially with age over a middle range (Gompertz 1825), and the fit fails at the oldest ages. Direct age standardisation compares death rates between populations, if age-specific rates are stable enough in each group and a standard population is chosen.
  - MS.O.A5.4.3 **Migration flows.** The gravity model predicts flows from the sizes of origin and destination and the distance between them. Hypothesis: the relation is empirical and fitted to past flows. It does not state why people move.
  - MS.O.A5.4.4 **Projection.** A cohort-component projection carries a population forward by age and sex from stated assumptions about fertility, mortality and migration. Stable population theory gives the long-run age structure and growth rate if age-specific rates stay constant in a closed population. A projection is a conditional statement, not a forecast.
- **MS.O.A5.5 Collective action and change.** **Collective action, the spread of practices and long-run change follow patterns that can be tested against dated records.** Key line (inductive; plural noun processes; dimension what moves through society; order from mobilisation to diffusion to long-run change). Members (3): mobilisation · diffusion · long-run change. Social movements that target a norm or an organisation. A movement contesting authority is PO.O.A3.3 (CONTESTED target rule). The free-rider result is EC.O.A5.3. Differentiation, diffusion, globalisation. Named theories of history (Marxian, functionalist) are answer-families, reconstructed under MS.M.3.
  - MS.O.A5.5.1 **Resource mobilisation.** Whether a movement emerges depends on organisation, resources and political opportunity more than on the level of grievance (McCarthy and Zald 1977). Grievance-based accounts are the rival (CONTESTED). Hypothesis: grievance is roughly constant across the cases compared. The free-rider result is EC.O.A5.3 (uses EC.O.A5.3).
  - MS.O.A5.5.2 **Diffusion of practices.** Cumulative adoption of an innovation often follows an S-shaped curve, and early adopters differ from late adopters (Ryan and Gross 1943; Rogers 1962). Hypothesis: adoption is not reversed, the population at risk is fixed and adoption dates are recorded. The curve describes timing and does not explain it.
  - MS.O.A5.5.3 **Theories of long-run change.** Marxian, functionalist and modernisation accounts of history each imply dated, checkable patterns, such as the form of class conflict or the growth of specialised institutions. They are tested against dated records (uses HI). Teleology and unfalsifiable formulations are the defeaters. OPEN: no account is established as general.
