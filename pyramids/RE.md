**Two-mode rule (binding, from the prototype).** Every RE sentence carries one mode. **·S (study)** owes W6, W7 or W8. **·T (theology)** owes W13, with W9 or W8. The ID is cell plus mode: RE.O.A1.1·T. W13 appears only in ·T.
- **RE-1 Tag.** Every RE sentence carries ·S or ·T.
- **RE-2 Separate warrants.** W13 never warrants a ·S sentence. W6 or W7 alone never warrants a ·T sentence.
- **RE-3 Report is not assertion.** A ·S sentence may report what a tradition teaches. Reporting is not adopting.
- **RE-4 Premises cross one way, labelled.** A ·T sentence may cite a ·S result as a premise. It must name the authority that makes the premise bear on doctrine.
- **RE-5 One sentence, one mode.** A sentence needing both modes is two sentences.
- **RE-6 Methodological agnosticism.** ·S brackets the truth of the ultimate claim (Smart; Berger's "methodological atheism", 1967, UNVERIFIED date). A ·S sentence that denies a doctrine has become a PL sentence and owes W9.

## 1. SCQA

**Situation.** Universities treat religion in two ways. Religious studies describes and compares religions from outside any one of them. Theology argues within a tradition from its authorities. **Complication.** No other pyramid owns either mode. PL declines religion as practice; MS files religion as a sector. LCC divides Christianity by topic into five subclasses but other traditions by name. A "world religions" bucket appears where schemes run out. **Question.** Which single partition places every religious claim exactly once, without merging the warrant of study with the warrant of faith? **Answer.** Four objects of the claim, each in two modes, with the tradition as substrate and the discipline as bridge.

## 2. Governing thought

Religion and theology is exhausted by four faces of one pyramid: objects, methods, warrants and bridges. Its objects are claims about four objects of the claim: the ultimate and the human condition, authoritative text, practice, and community and institution.

## 3. Key line

- **Logic.** Inductive.
- **Plural noun.** objects of the claim.
- **Dimension.** What the sentence is about.
- **Order.** Structural, from the referent to its carriers: what is ultimate, the text that discloses it, the practice that answers it, the community that carries it.

1. The ultimate and the human condition
2. Authoritative text
3. Practice
4. Community and institution

**MECE test.** Exclusive: by the subject rule, each sentence has one subject. "Genesis 1 is a priestly composition" is about a text (A2). "God created from nothing" is about the ultimate (A1), even when its warrant is a text. Exhaustive: religious experience is A3.2 as discipline, or MS.O.A1 as mental process. Sacred place is A1.2 (status), A3.1 (use in rite) or A4.1 (governance). Ethics and religious law are A3.3. Oral text is inside A2, which prevents a bias toward written scripture.

**CONTESTED (order).** An anthropological order would run community first. The order is structural, not a ranking.

## 4. Face O — Objects

### RE.O.A1 The ultimate and the human condition

**SCQA.** *S.* Traditions make claims about God, gods, Brahman, emptiness or atua, about the world's dependence, and about human ends. Both study and theology report or assert such claims. *C.* Theism, nondualism and Buddhist analysis look like rival departments, and each uses its own vocabulary. They answer three questions in common. The questions differ by what the claim relates to, not by which tradition asks them. *Q.* Which doctrinal questions are distinct? *A.* Three, by the relatum of the claim: the ultimate itself, the world's relation to it, and the human end.

**Key line.** Inductive · plural noun: doctrinal questions · dimension: the relatum of the claim · order: structural, the ultimate, the world, the person. Members (3).
1. **RE.O.A1.1 The ultimate itself** claims what is ultimate and what may be said of it.
2. **RE.O.A1.2 The world's relation to the ultimate** claims creation, emanation, cosmogony, providence, revelation and sacred status.
3. **RE.O.A1.3 The human end** claims the human condition and its goal: sin and salvation, bondage and liberation, death and after.

**Sub-cell claims.**
- **RE.O.A1.1 The ultimate itself.** **A claim here states what is ultimate and what may be said of it, and it is ·S when reported and ·T when asserted.** The cell holds three kinds of answer. Some give positive attributes (RE.O.A1.1.2). Some limit speech to negation (RE.O.A1.1.4, RE.O.A1.1.7). Madhyamaka holds that things lack intrinsic nature (RE.O.A1.1.6). ·S: the kind of answer is decided by which predicates the source permits. A source that permits no positive predicate is not a theistic attribute claim. An argument for or against the ultimate from premises any reasoner may grant is PL.O.A1.1, not this cell.
- **RE.O.A1.2 The world's relation to the ultimate.** **A claim here states whether and how the world depends on the ultimate, and what status that gives to persons, places and things.** Creation ex nihilo (RE.O.A1.2.1) posits a maker distinct from the world. Whakapapa cosmogony (RE.O.A1.2.2) posits descent from named primal parents. ·S: the two are compared only after each is described in its own terms. Tapu and noa (RE.O.A1.2.3) are statuses, not acts. How a status is applied at a particular place is practice (RE.O.A3). The physical age of the world is a claim for EA.
- **RE.O.A1.3 The human end.** **A claim here states the human condition, the goal and the route between them, and it differs by who is held to act to reach the goal.** Karma and saṃsāra (RE.O.A1.3.1) place the action chiefly with the person. Grace and synergy (RE.O.A1.3.2) divide it between the divine and the will. The beatific vision (RE.O.A1.3.3) names the goal as direct knowledge of the ultimate. ·S: what adherents expect after death is a report. Whether it occurs is ·T or outside RE. The psychology of belief in an afterlife is MS.O.A1.

**Named objects.**
- **RE.O.A1.1.1 The Trinity**, defined at Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381). As true: ·T. Its conciliar history: ·S.
- **RE.O.A1.1.2 Tawḥīd and the divine attributes.** Ashʿarī, Māturīdī and Muʿtazilī answers differ.
- **RE.O.A1.1.3 Createdness of the Qurʾan** and the *miḥna* (from 833, UNVERIFIED date). Muʿtazilīs held it created; Ashʿarīs uncreated.
- **RE.O.A1.1.4 Apophatic theology.** God is known by negation. Pseudo-Dionysius (c. 500); Maimonides' negative attributes.
- **RE.O.A1.1.5 Nirguṇa and saguṇa Brahman.** Advaita (Śaṅkara, c. 8th century) against Viśiṣṭādvaita (Rāmānuja).
- **RE.O.A1.1.6 Śūnyatā** (Nāgārjuna, c. 2nd century) as a Madhyamaka claim. Its argument alone, from shared premises, is PL.
- **RE.O.A1.1.7 Ein Sof** in Kabbalah.
- **RE.O.A1.1.8 Io as supreme being** in some Māori traditions (·S). CONTESTED: its pre-contact antiquity is disputed. See T/p2_ANZSRC45.md.
- **RE.O.A1.1.9 Otto's numinous** (*Das Heilige*, 1917, UNVERIFIED date) as a ·S theory of the sacred.
- **RE.O.A1.2.1 Creation ex nihilo.**
- **RE.O.A1.2.2 Whakapapa cosmogony** from Ranginui and Papatūānuku. Human descent from a named tupuna is HI.O.A1.
- **RE.O.A1.2.3 Tapu and noa** as statuses of persons, places and things.
- **RE.O.A1.3.1 Karma and saṃsāra; mokṣa; nirvāṇa.**
- **RE.O.A1.3.2 Grace and synergy.** Salvation by grace alone against cooperation of grace and will.
- **RE.O.A1.3.3 The beatific vision.**
- **RE.O.A1.0.1 The axial age** (Jaspers, 1949: roughly 8th to 3rd centuries BCE). A ·S comparative claim (W6) of a shift toward transcendence. CONTESTED: critics find no common denominator and no clean break. Alternative home: HI.O.A2 (periodisation). Filed at A-level because it spans A1.1–A1.3.

**Hypotheses and tests.**
- **Otto's numinous (RE.O.A1.1.9)** holds as a ·S theory if religious experience has a recognisable common structure across traditions. Critics deny the common structure. It is tested against first-person reports, not against doctrine.
- **The axial age (RE.O.A1.0.1)** holds if several civilisations show a shift toward transcendence in the stated centuries, without a common source. It fails if the shifts are neither comparable nor contemporary. CONTESTED, as above.
- **A ·S comparison of apophatic theology (RE.O.A1.1.4) with Madhyamaka (RE.O.A1.1.6)** holds only if each is first described in its own terms (RE.M.B4). It does not show that the two make the same claim.
- **Creation ex nihilo (RE.O.A1.2.1).** Whether the doctrine is present in the Hebrew Bible, or arose in later interpretation, is CONTESTED among scholars. UNVERIFIED as to the period usually proposed.
- **Mode check.** A sentence that a doctrine is false is not ·S (RE-6). It is a PL sentence and owes W9.

### RE.O.A2 Authoritative text

The cell covers written and oral text alike.

**SCQA.** *S.* Traditions hold some texts as scripture or authoritative tradition. Some are written and some are recited. Texts are ranked, copied, fixed and read. *C.* Canon studies, textual criticism and exegesis are separate fields. Each follows one text through a different part of its life. Written and oral transmission are often studied apart. *Q.* Which text claims are distinct? *A.* Three, by stage of the text's life: its status, its establishment and its meaning.

**Key line.** Inductive · plural noun: text claims · dimension: the stage of the text's life · order: chronological, status, transmission, interpretation. Members (3).
1. **RE.O.A2.1 Canon and status** claims which texts count as authoritative, and in what rank.
2. **RE.O.A2.2 Transmission and establishment** claims how the text was composed, transmitted and fixed.
3. **RE.O.A2.3 Interpretation** claims what the text means.

**Sub-cell claims.**
- **RE.O.A2.1 Canon and status.** **A claim here states which texts a community treats as authoritative and in what rank, and the extent of a canon is a fact about that community's reception.** Canons differ in extent (RE.O.A2.1.1), in the mode of reception, heard or remembered (RE.O.A2.1.2), and in division (RE.O.A2.1.3). ·S: the extent of a canon at a stated date is a dated fact (W7). The event of its closing is HI.O.A1. ·T: whether a text ought to be in the canon is a W13 claim. Canon closure was a process in some traditions, not one act. The claim is tested against the sources of each tradition.
- **RE.O.A2.2 Transmission and establishment.** **A claim here states how a text was composed, copied and fixed, and it treats the text as text (·S).** Written witnesses (RE.O.A2.2.1, RE.O.A2.2.3, RE.O.A2.2.4) are tested by collation. Chains of report (RE.O.A2.2.2) are tested by criticism of the transmitters. Recitation (RE.O.A2.2.5, RE.O.A2.2.6) is tested by comparing independent reciters' versions. A variant reading is a claim about a text. The forgery or interpolation of a text is HI.O.A1.4. The language variety of a witness is LN.O.A5. The number of witnesses is not a measure of their weight.
- **RE.O.A2.3 Interpretation.** **A claim here states what a text means under a stated set of rules, and it is ·S when historical and ·T when made within the tradition.** The fourfold sense (RE.O.A2.3.1), tafsīr (RE.O.A2.3.2), midrash and PaRDeS (RE.O.A2.3.3) and Mīmāṃsā (RE.O.A2.3.4) each supply rules. ·S: reports the rules and compares what they yield. ·T: applies them to a passage. A meaning claim is tested against the rules it names. One passage can bear different meanings under different rules without contradiction in ·S. The meaning of a word in general is LN.O.A3.

**Second-level key line for RE.O.A2.2.** Inductive · plural noun: transmission claims · dimension: the carrier of the text · order: structural, written, reported, recited. Members (3).
1. **Written witnesses** are RE.O.A2.2.1, RE.O.A2.2.3 and RE.O.A2.2.4.
2. **Chains of report** are RE.O.A2.2.2.
3. **Recitation** is RE.O.A2.2.5 and RE.O.A2.2.6.

Order note: canon is listed first because rank is what makes a text an object here. CONTESTED: historically, composition precedes canon; the alternative order is A2.2, A2.1, A2.3.

**Named objects.**
- **RE.O.A2.1.1 Biblical canons.** The Tanakh; Protestant, Catholic (with deuterocanonical books) and Ethiopian Orthodox canons differ in extent.
- **RE.O.A2.1.2 Śruti and smṛti.** Heard and remembered texts in Hindu traditions.
- **RE.O.A2.1.3 Pāli Tipiṭaka**, written down in Sri Lanka c. 1st century BCE; the Chinese and Tibetan canons.
- **RE.O.A2.2.1 The ʿUthmānic codex** (c. 650) and the *qirāʾāt* (canonical readings).
- **RE.O.A2.2.2 Ḥadīth and *isnād* criticism.**
- **RE.O.A2.2.3 Dead Sea Scrolls** (found from 1947) as textual witnesses (·S).
- **RE.O.A2.2.4 Mishnah** (c. 200 CE) and the Jerusalem and Babylonian Talmuds.
- **RE.O.A2.2.5 Vedic oral transmission** by fixed recitation patterns.
- **RE.O.A2.2.6 Karakia and kōrero tuku iho** as oral text, transmitted in whare wānanga. Placement subject to Māori-led review (T/p2_ANZSRC45.md).
- **RE.O.A2.3.1 Four senses of scripture:** literal, allegorical, moral and anagogical. Origen gave the first three; Cassian added the anagogical. As a claim about how scripture means: ·T. Its use as a procedure is RE.M.B2.
- **RE.O.A2.3.2 Tafsīr *bi'l-maʾthūr* and *bi'l-raʾy*:** by transmitted report and by reasoned opinion.
- **RE.O.A2.3.3 Midrash and PaRDeS.**
- **RE.O.A2.3.4 Mīmāṃsā rules** of Vedic interpretation.

**Hypotheses and tests.**
- **Textual criticism (RE.M.B1)** holds if the surviving witnesses descend from earlier copies and their errors are of recognisable kinds. A family tree of witnesses holds only if each was copied from one exemplar. Mixing of exemplars defeats it.
- **Ḥadīth and *isnād* criticism (RE.O.A2.2.2)** holds if the transmitters can be identified and their reliability assessed from other sources. How early the chains were formed is CONTESTED between sceptical and accepting scholars.
- **The Dead Sea Scrolls (RE.O.A2.2.3)** are witnesses to a text only if they are dated by a method independent of their content. Their readings are compared with later witnesses (·S).
- **Vedic recitation (RE.O.A2.2.5)** preserves a text if fixed patterns prevent change. That the patterns were in use at an early date is a claim for HI, and UNVERIFIED here.
- **Four senses of scripture (RE.O.A2.3.1)** is a ·T claim that scripture bears several meanings. The history of the doctrine is ·S.

### RE.O.A3 Practice

**SCQA.** *S.* Adherents worship, pray, fast, meditate, keep law and live by codes. These acts are observed by outsiders and prescribed by insiders. *C.* Liturgics, spirituality, ethics and religious law are separate departments. A single act, such as a fast, can belong to more than one of them. *Q.* Which practice claims are distinct? *A.* Three, by whom the act addresses: the sacred, the self or others.

**Key line.** Inductive · plural noun: practice claims · dimension: whom the act addresses · order: structural, the sacred, the self, others. Members (3).
1. **RE.O.A3.1 Worship and rite** claims acts addressed to the sacred.
2. **RE.O.A3.2 Discipline and formation** claims acts addressed to transforming the self.
3. **RE.O.A3.3 Conduct and law** claims acts addressed to others, and the norms that govern them.

**Sub-cell claims.**
- **RE.O.A3.1 Worship and rite.** **A claim here states an act addressed to the sacred and what the act is held to do, and the form of the act is separate from its meaning.** Sacrament (RE.O.A3.1.1), ṣalāt and ḥajj (RE.O.A3.1.2) and pūjā (RE.O.A3.1.3) are acts with a prescribed form. ·S: the form is observed (W4) and the meaning is reported by participants (W8). The two can differ, and the difference is a finding. ·T: what the act effects is a W13 claim. The history of a rite as a dated event is HI. The form of a ritual object is AR.O.A2.
- **RE.O.A3.2 Discipline and formation.** **A claim here states an act addressed to changing the self and the change the tradition expects.** Meditation (RE.O.A3.2.1), a monastic rule (RE.O.A3.2.2) and bhakti (RE.O.A3.2.3) differ in method: attention, daily order or devotion. ·S: what practitioners do is observed. Measured effects on brain or mood are MS.O.A2 and MS.O.A1. A measured effect does not establish the tradition's stated aim. The stated aim is ·T.
- **RE.O.A3.3 Conduct and law.** **A claim here states a norm governing acts toward others as a tradition holds it, and the norm is tested against that tradition's sources and rules.** Fiqh rulings (RE.O.A3.3.1) and halakha (RE.O.A3.3.2) are derived by the method of a law school. Natural law in Aquinas (RE.O.A3.3.3) is derived from an account of reason and created order. ·T: a ruling is tested by whether it follows from the school's sources by its rules. ·S: that a school holds a ruling is a report. A civil duty that results is LA.O.A1. A moral argument from shared premises is PL.O.A4.

The dimension is attested inside traditions. Fiqh divides *ʿibādāt* (worship) from *muʿāmalāt* (dealings). Jewish thought divides duties toward God from duties toward others. The pyramid uses the division as a dimension, not as an authority. An act addressed to two is filed by the claim's subject. Ramadan fasting as a duty toward God is A3.1; as self-formation it is A3.2. CONTESTED: the alternative files such acts once, under the tradition's own primary label.

**Named objects.**
- **RE.O.A3.1.1 Eucharist and sacrament.**
- **RE.O.A3.1.2 Ṣalāt and ḥajj.**
- **RE.O.A3.1.3 Pūjā and śrauta ritual.**
- **RE.O.A3.1.4 Karakia in performance; pōwhiri; tangihanga.** Subject to Māori-led review.
- **RE.O.A3.1.5 Rites of passage** (van Gennep, 1909, UNVERIFIED date): separation, transition, incorporation. A ·S comparative claim.
- **RE.O.A3.1.6 Veneration of icons** and the ruling of Nicaea II (787). The icon's form and making are AR.O.A1 and AR.O.A2.
- **RE.O.A3.2.1 Meditation** (*śamatha*, *vipaśyanā*); yoga; dhikr. Its neural correlates are MS.O.A2.
- **RE.O.A3.2.2 The Rule of Benedict** (c. 530); musar.
- **RE.O.A3.2.3 Bhakti** as devotional discipline.
- **RE.O.A3.3.1 Fiqh rulings in *muʿāmalāt*.**
- **RE.O.A3.3.2 Halakha** in the Shulchan Aruch (printed 1565, UNVERIFIED date); dharmaśāstra.
- **RE.O.A3.3.3 Natural law in Aquinas** (*Summa* I–II q. 94) as moral theology (·T). The same argument from shared premises is PL.O.A4.
- **RE.O.A3.3.4 Kashrut and halal.** Filed by subject: as worship A3.1, as conduct A3.3.
- **RE.O.A3.3.5 Tikanga as correct practice** before atua and people; manaakitanga as obligation to guests. Tikanga as part of NZ law is LA.O.A1 (*Ellis v R* [2022] NZSC 114).

**Hypotheses and tests.**
- **Rites of passage (RE.O.A3.1.5)** hold as a ·S schema where a rite marks a change of social status. The claim is not that every rite has three phases. The schema fails for a rite that marks no change of status.
- **Meditation (RE.O.A3.2.1)** is described in ·S by instructions and observed practice. Terms such as *śamatha* and *vipaśyanā* are used differently across schools. A comparison must say which school's usage it follows.
- **Natural law in Aquinas (RE.O.A3.3.3)** holds in ·T for those who grant that a created order is knowable by reason. Without that premise the argument is a PL sentence.
- **Kashrut and halal (RE.O.A3.3.4)** are filed by subject. A rule on slaughter as an act of worship is A3.1. As a rule of conduct it is A3.3.
- **Fiqh and halakha** both rely on a hierarchy of sources. A ruling's standing depends on the school consulted, so a ·S report names the school.

### RE.O.A4 Community and institution

**SCQA.** *S.* Religions are carried by communities with offices, members and histories. Each community holds its own account of who may speak for it. *C.* Ecclesiology, church history and sociology of religion overlap. The tradition's own order differs from the general sociology of organisations. *Q.* Which community claims are distinct? *A.* Three, by what holds the community together: authority, boundary and continuity.

**Key line.** Inductive · plural noun: community claims · dimension: what holds the community together · order: structural, authority, boundary, continuity. Members (3).
1. **RE.O.A4.1 Authority and office** claims who may teach, rule, ordain or decide.
2. **RE.O.A4.2 Membership and boundary** claims who belongs, and what puts a member or teaching outside.
3. **RE.O.A4.3 Continuity and division** claims succession, lineage, reform and schism.

**Sub-cell claims.**
- **RE.O.A4.1 Authority and office.** **A claim here states who may teach, rule, ordain or decide in a community, by that community's own rule of recognition (·T) or as reported (·S).** Papacy and councils (RE.O.A4.1.1), the ʿulamāʾ and *madhhabs* (RE.O.A4.1.2) and the rabbinate (RE.O.A4.1.3) rest on different sources of authority: office, learning or a rule of life. ·S: how authority works in practice is observed and may differ from the stated rule. The general sociology of organisations is MS.O.A5.
- **RE.O.A4.2 Membership and boundary.** **A claim here states who belongs and what places a member or teaching outside, and the line is drawn by an authority that the claim must name.** Excommunication and *takfīr* (RE.O.A4.2.1) are acts of an authority. Orthodoxy and heresy (RE.O.A4.2.2) are labels used by insiders. ·S: such labels are reported, not applied. A ·S sentence that calls a group heretical has taken a side and is ·T (RE-2). Self-identification in a survey is MS.O.A5.
- **RE.O.A4.3 Continuity and division.** **A claim here states how a community holds that it continues, through succession or lineage, and how it divides, through reform or schism.** Apostolic succession (RE.O.A4.3.1) and *paramparā* (RE.O.A4.3.2) are claims of continuity. The divisions of 1054, the sixteenth century and 632 (RE.O.A4.3.3) are claims of rupture. ·S: a dated record of succession is HI.O.A1 evidence. ·T: whether a succession is valid is a W13 claim made within the community.

**Named objects.**
- **RE.O.A4.1.1 Papacy and councils.**
- **RE.O.A4.1.2 The ʿulamāʾ and the four Sunni *madhhabs*; the Twelver imamate.**
- **RE.O.A4.1.3 The rabbinate and the *posek*.**
- **RE.O.A4.1.4 Saṅgha and *vinaya*** as the monastic community's rule. Its membership rules are filed at A4.2 by subject.
- **RE.O.A4.1.5 Tohunga and kaumātua; marae *kawa***, which varies by iwi and hapū. Subject to Māori-led review.
- **RE.O.A4.2.1 Excommunication, *ḥerem*, *takfīr*.**
- **RE.O.A4.2.2 Orthodoxy and heresy.** In ·S, insider labels are marked as reported.
- **RE.O.A4.3.1 Apostolic succession.**
- **RE.O.A4.3.2 Guru-śiṣya *paramparā*; Śaṅkara's *maṭhas*.**
- **RE.O.A4.3.3 The East–West schism (1054); the Reformation (from 1517); the Sunni–Shīʿa division after 632** (·S).
- **RE.O.A4.3.4 Ringatū** (Te Kooti, c. 1868, UNVERIFIED date) and **the Rātana Church** (1925, UNVERIFIED date) as Māori Christian communities.
- **RE.O.A4.3.5 The "gate of ijtihād closed" thesis**, rejected as history by Hallaq, "Was the Gate of Ijtihad Closed?" (*IJMES* 16, 1984) (·S).

**Hypotheses and tests.**
- **Apostolic succession (RE.O.A4.3.1)** holds in ·T if office is conferred by a bishop in an unbroken chain. Its ·S counterpart is the dated record of lists of bishops, and the early lists are CONTESTED.
- **The "gate of ijtihād closed" thesis (RE.O.A4.3.5)** claims that independent legal reasoning ended by a stated century, usually given as the fourth of the Hijra (UNVERIFIED). It fails if legal works after that date show reasoning beyond a school's earlier rulings. Hallaq argued that they do.
- **Excommunication, *ḥerem* and *takfīr* (RE.O.A4.2.1)** are acts of an authority recognised in the community. Whether the act was valid is ·T. That it occurred, and when, is a dated record.
- **The Sunni–Shīʿa division after 632 (RE.O.A4.3.3)** is a ·S claim. Each community's account of its own origin is reported as held, not adopted (RE-3).

## 5. Face M — Methods

Each role is used in both modes. The mode is set by the warrant the claim owes, not by the method.

| ID | Role | Shared role | ·S example | ·T example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RE.M.B1 | Establish the text | M3 Observe, M6 | textual criticism, manuscript study | fixing the received text for use |
| RE.M.B2 | Interpret | M2 Derive, M6 | historical-critical exegesis | exegesis within the tradition's rules (four senses, tafsīr) |
| RE.M.B3 | Observe practice | M3 Observe | ethnography, surveys of belief | pastoral observation |

## 8. Assignment rules
1. Place by the subject of the sentence (the ultimate, the text, the practice, the community), then by mode.
2. ·S and ·T are never merged. The same sentence, asserted as true within a tradition, is ·T. Reported as held, it is ·S.
3. An argument from premises any reasoner may grant (cosmological, ontological, problem of evil) is PL. The same argument from scripture is RE ·T.
4. The dated event of a tradition's past is HI, cited by RE ·S.
5. A civil duty arising from a religion is LA. A canon-internal duty is RE ·T.
6. The neural or psychological process of religious experience is MS. The discipline as practice is RE.O.A3.2.
7. A measurement offered as doctrine is refused. Its measurement goes to its science; the doctrine stays in ·T without the measurement's warrant.
8. Comparison does not inherit any canon's W13.
9. Māori and other Indigenous religious claims follow p2_ANZSRC45.md and need Māori-led review.
10. Pilot repair (claim 19). A dated event in a text's history (when the Mishnah was redacted) is HI.O.A1. RE.O.A2.2 keeps claims about the transmitted text as text: variants, recensions and readings.
11. Pilot repair (claim 46). Reporting what a theologian held is RE·S. Evaluating an argument whose premises any reasoner may grant is PL, even when a theologian made it.
