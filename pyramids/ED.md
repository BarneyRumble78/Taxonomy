# ED — Education

New field, added at the owner's direction. Standard v2 cited, not copied. Why it is here: the physics audit had nowhere to put physics education research, and MSC 97 treats mathematics as the object of a learning claim. Nothing owned the learning claim itself.

## 1. SCQA
**Situation.** Education research states what people learn, under which arrangements, how it is measured, and how schools and systems organise it.
**Complication.** v1 scattered these claims. Physics education went to MS, MSC 97 sat in MA, and JEL A2 and ANZSRC 39 had no home.
**Question.** By what dimension can every claim about learning and teaching be placed once, with the subject matter left to its owner?
**Answer.** By the element of the educational arrangement the claim is about: aims, learners, teaching, assessment, or institutions.

## 2. Governing thought
Education is exhausted by four faces of one pyramid: objects, methods, warrants and bridges.
Its objects divide into five elements of an educational arrangement: aims and curricula, learners, teaching, assessment, and institutions and systems.

## 3. Key line
Dimension: **the element of an educational arrangement.** Order: from purpose to system.
1. Aims and curricula
2. Learners
3. Teaching
4. Assessment
5. Institutions and systems

## 4. Face O — Objects

Each A-cell gives an SCQA, a key line, sub-cells that open with a bold claim, child cells one level down, and its named objects. Effect sizes and dates below are quoted as reported. Those not checked against the source are marked UNVERIFIED.

### ED.O.A1 Aims and curricula

**SCQA.** Situation. Every educational arrangement serves a stated aim and selects content from what a society could teach. Complication. Aims are normative, content belongs to its discipline, and a curriculum is a policy document. One sentence often mixes all three. Question. Which claims about aims and curricula does education own? Answer. Education owns the statement of an adopted aim, the selection and ordering of content as an arrangement, learning progressions, and the contents of named frameworks. The argument for an aim is PL, the truth of content is its owner's, and the decision to adopt a curriculum is PO.

**Key line** (inductive; plural noun: curriculum questions; dimension: the question asked of the curriculum; order: from purpose to a named framework). Members (4): Aims · Curriculum content · Sequence and progression · National curricula.

- ED.O.A1.1 Aims — **An adopted aim is a stated position of a body with authority. A claim about what it says is checked against its text.** The stated purposes of education. The normative argument for them is PL; the statement of an adopted aim is ED (W12).
  - ED.O.A1.1.1 Stated aims of a framework. The stated aims of a framework can be read from its text and compared across jurisdictions. A claim that a system pursues an aim needs evidence of practice beyond the text (uses PO.O.A4.3).
  - ED.O.A1.1.2 Aim families. Stated aims are commonly grouped as preparation for work, for citizenship and for personal development. A sentence must say which family it assumes. The relative weight of the families is a value claim (uses PL.O.A4).
  - ED.O.A1.1.3 Aims and what is measured. An aim that no instrument measures tends to receive less assessed time than a measured aim. The size of this tendency is OPEN. It links to ED.O.A4.4.2.
- ED.O.A1.2 Curriculum content — **The choice and weighting of content is an educational claim, and the truth of that content stays with its owner.** What is taught. The truth of the content stays with its owner (a theorem is MA).
  - ED.O.A1.2.1 Intended, implemented and attained curriculum. The curriculum a system specifies, the curriculum teachers deliver and the curriculum students learn differ in measurable ways. TIMSS uses this three-level distinction. Each level needs its own evidence: documents, teacher reports, test results.
  - ED.O.A1.2.2 Opportunity to learn. Other things equal, student scores on a topic rise with the time and coverage given to it. The claim fails where coverage is measured only by teacher self-report with no check.
  - ED.O.A1.2.3 Simple view of reading. Gough and Tunmer (1986) model reading comprehension as the product of decoding and language comprehension. Hypotheses: the two components are measured separately; each ranges from zero upward; the product form is a modelling choice tested against additive forms. It implies a reading curriculum must cover both.
- ED.O.A1.3 Sequence and progression — **A stated order of topics is a hypothesis about how learners reach understanding. It can fail.** Learning progressions and prerequisites.
  - ED.O.A1.3.1 Prerequisite structure. Topic A is a prerequisite of topic B if learners lacking A are less likely to master B. Teaching A first must also raise success on B. The first condition is correlational. The second needs a trial.
  - ED.O.A1.3.2 Learning progressions. A progression states ordered levels of understanding in a domain, supported by longitudinal or cross-sectional data. Different instruction can produce different orders, so a progression is tied to the teaching under which it was observed (W4).
  - ED.O.A1.3.3 Spiral curriculum. Bruner (1960) proposed returning to a topic at increasing depth. The claim that revisiting beats a single pass for retention and understanding is testable. It overlaps spacing (ED.O.A3.3.1).
- ED.O.A1.4 National curricula — **A national curriculum is a stated framework (W12), and a claim about its content is settled by its text.** E.g. Te Whāriki (NZ early childhood) and Te Marautanga o Aotearoa (Māori-medium) as stated frameworks (W12).
  - ED.O.A1.4.1 The New Zealand Curriculum (2007). It states values, five key competencies and eight learning areas. The competencies are thinking; using language, symbols and texts; managing self; relating to others; and participating and contributing. The learning areas are English, the arts, health and physical education, learning languages, mathematics and statistics, science, social sciences, and technology.
  - ED.O.A1.4.2 Australian Curriculum general capabilities. The Australian Curriculum states seven general capabilities beside its learning areas (UNVERIFIED for the current version). They are literacy, numeracy, information and communication technology, critical and creative thinking, personal and social capability, ethical understanding, and intercultural understanding.
  - ED.O.A1.4.3 Level-based and year-based frameworks. A framework can state achievement objectives by level, as the NZ Curriculum does across levels 1 to 8. It can instead state content by year, as the Australian Curriculum does. The two designs make different claims about when a topic is taught.

Named objects: aims of education (W12), intended, implemented and attained curriculum, opportunity to learn, simple view of reading (hypotheses: separable components, product form), learning progression (hypothesis: order holds under the stated instruction), prerequisite relation, spiral curriculum, The New Zealand Curriculum (2007), key competencies, general capabilities.

### ED.O.A2 Learners

**SCQA.** Situation. Learning happens in a person who arrives with knowledge, beliefs, motives and circumstances. Complication. The mechanism of mind belongs to MS, group labels invite reification, and many headline effects have weak replication records. Question. Which claims about the learner inside an arrangement does education own? Answer. Education owns what learners bring to an arrangement and how they change within it. That is prior knowledge and misconceptions, readiness, motivation, and diversity and need. The cognitive mechanism, the developmental theory and the social pattern are cited from MS.

**Key line** (inductive; plural noun: learner attributes; dimension: what the learner brings or becomes; order: knowledge, then readiness, then drive, then difference). Members (4): Prior knowledge and misconceptions · Development · Motivation · Diversity and learning need.

- ED.O.A2.1 Prior knowledge and misconceptions — **What learners already believe about a topic predicts what they gain from teaching. Some beliefs survive ordinary instruction.** E.g. the impetus misconception in force and motion (physics education research). The cognitive mechanism is MS.
  - ED.O.A2.1.1 Prior knowledge effect. Measured prior knowledge of a topic predicts later attainment on that topic beyond general ability, in many studies. The effect is topic-specific and is not shown to transfer to other topics.
  - ED.O.A2.1.2 Force Concept Inventory. Hestenes, Wells and Swackhammer (1992) built a test whose wrong options are common beliefs about force and motion. Hypotheses: the options match beliefs in the tested population; items are read as intended; scores are used for groups, not for certifying a person. The physics content is PH.
  - ED.O.A2.1.3 Conceptual change. Posner, Strike, Hewson and Gertzog (1982) hold that a learner replaces a conception only under two conditions. The learner is dissatisfied with it, and the new one is intelligible, plausible and fruitful. Hypotheses: the prior conception is coherent; the learner weighs rival conceptions. Whether misconceptions are coherent theories or loose fragments is CONTESTED (diSessa 1993).
- ED.O.A2.2 Development — **The age at which a topic can be taught depends on prior learning as well as age. It is an empirical question, not a stage fact.** Age-related readiness, cited from MS.
  - ED.O.A2.2.1 Zone of proximal development. Vygotsky described the gap between what a learner does alone and with help. Scaffolding (Wood, Bruner and Ross 1976) assumes help is matched to the learner and withdrawn as competence grows. The development theory is MS (uses MS.O.A3.3).
  - ED.O.A2.2.2 Stage theories in teaching. Stage theories are used to set readiness (uses MS.O.A3.3.1). Evidence that younger children succeed on a topic with suitable instruction weakens a stage as a strict limit. The strength of that weakening is CONTESTED.
  - ED.O.A2.2.3 Cumulative advantage in reading. Stanovich (1986) argued that early reading differences widen over time, because better readers read more. Whether gaps widen across measures and samples is CONTESTED.
- ED.O.A2.3 Motivation and engagement — **Motivation is measured by self-report and by behaviour. Its causal effect on attainment is harder to identify than its association.** The mechanism is MS.
  - ED.O.A2.3.1 Self-determination theory. Deci and Ryan hold that autonomy, competence and relatedness support more self-determined motivation. Hypotheses: motivation varies along a continuum from external control to intrinsic interest; support for the three needs is manipulable in class. The mechanism is MS (uses MS.O.A1.4.2).
  - ED.O.A2.3.2 Expectancy–value model. Eccles and Wigfield hold that choice, effort and persistence follow expected success and task value. Hypotheses: expectancy and value are measured separately; both relate to later course choice.
  - ED.O.A2.3.3 Mindset interventions. Brief interventions on beliefs about the malleability of ability raised outcomes in some trials. Average effects in meta-analyses are small and their size is CONTESTED (UNVERIFIED as a figure).
- ED.O.A2.4 Diversity and learning need — **Average attainment differences between groups are descriptive, and a claim about their cause needs an identified design.** Inclusion, special education, gifted learners.
  - ED.O.A2.4.1 Identification thresholds. A learning need such as a reading disability is identified by a threshold on a continuous distribution. The threshold is a convention (W12), so one learner can be identified in one system and not in another.
  - ED.O.A2.4.2 Response to intervention. A learner can be identified by inadequate progress under a research-based intervention, not by one score. Hypotheses: the intervention is delivered as designed; progress is measured reliably.
  - ED.O.A2.4.3 Learning styles. Matching teaching to a learner's stated style does not raise outcomes in the designs that cross style with method. Pashler et al. (2008) found no adequate evidence for this meshing hypothesis. A valid test needs a crossover interaction.
  - ED.O.A2.4.4 Stereotype threat. Steele and Aronson (1995) reported lower test scores for members of a stereotyped group under a diagnostic framing. The size and replication of the effect are CONTESTED. The social mechanism is MS (uses MS.O.A4.5); its effect on a stated assessment is ED.

Named objects: Force Concept Inventory (hypotheses: options match beliefs, group use), conceptual change model (four conditions), zone of proximal development, scaffolding, cumulative advantage in reading, self-determination theory (continuum of motivation, three needs), expectancy–value model, response to intervention, meshing hypothesis, stereotype threat.

### ED.O.A3 Teaching

**SCQA.** Situation. Teaching is the element of an arrangement that can be changed most directly, so most intervention claims sit here. Complication. An effect depends on the learners, the content, the delivery and the outcome measure. Effect sizes from different designs are not comparable, and "what works" hides these conditions. Question. Which teaching claims does education own, and under which conditions? Answer. Education owns causal claims that an arrangement changed learning, each stated with its learners, content, delivery and measure. The memory mechanism is MS and the technology artefact is CS.

**Key line** (inductive; plural noun: teaching arrangements; dimension: the part of the teaching act the claim is about; order: what is presented, then the response, then practice, then the medium, then the cultural frame). Members (5): Instruction · Feedback · Practice and retrieval · Technology in teaching · Culturally sustaining pedagogy.

- ED.O.A3.1 Instruction — **Explicit instruction outperforms minimally guided discovery for novices, and the advantage shrinks or reverses as expertise grows.** Explicit instruction versus discovery; worked examples help novices (an expertise-reversal effect is a stated defeater for experts).
  - ED.O.A3.1.1 Cognitive load theory. Sweller (1988) proposed a model of design for learning. Hypotheses: working memory is limited for new material; long-term memory stores schemas without that limit; learning is schema construction. Extraneous load can be cut by design. The memory limit is MS (uses MS.O.A1.3.4).
  - ED.O.A3.1.2 Worked-example and expertise reversal. Novices learn more from studying worked examples than from solving the same problems unaided. For learners with more expertise the advantage fades and can reverse (Kalyuga and others 2003). The boundary depends on measured prior knowledge.
  - ED.O.A3.1.3 Guidance debate. Kirschner, Sweller and Clark (2006) argued that minimal guidance fails. Hmelo-Silver, Duncan and Chinn (2007) replied that problem-based and inquiry designs are heavily guided. Whether a named inquiry design counts as minimal guidance is CONTESTED.
  - ED.O.A3.1.4 Active learning in undergraduate STEM. Freeman and colleagues (2014) pooled studies and reported higher examination scores and fewer failures under active learning than under lecture. The mean was about 0.47 standard deviations (UNVERIFIED). Hake (1998) used the normalised gain g = (post − pre) / (100 − pre) on class means. Hypotheses: same instrument before and after; comparable classes.
- ED.O.A3.2 Feedback — **Feedback changes performance in either direction, and the direction depends on its content, timing and the learner's task.** Effects vary widely by type; some feedback lowers performance (UNVERIFIED as a meta-analytic figure).
  - ED.O.A3.2.1 Feedback interventions. Kluger and DeNisi (1996) reported that feedback lowered performance in more than a third of the effects they pooled (UNVERIFIED figure). Their theory predicts weaker effects when feedback draws attention to the self than to the task.
  - ED.O.A3.2.2 Levels of feedback. Hattie and Timperley (2007) separate feedback about the task, the process, self-regulation and the self. The claim that process feedback beats praise of the self is testable. Their mean effect sizes are UNVERIFIED here.
  - ED.O.A3.2.3 Timing of feedback. Immediate feedback may suit simple recall, and delayed feedback may suit transfer. Reviews report mixed results, so the best timing is CONTESTED.
- ED.O.A3.3 Practice and retrieval — **Spaced practice and recall practice each improve delayed retention compared with massed study and rereading.** Spacing and retrieval practice improve long-term retention. The memory mechanism is MS.
  - ED.O.A3.3.1 Spacing effect. Cepeda and colleagues (2006) pooled studies of distributed practice. Retention rose with spacing, and the best gap depended on the retention interval. Hypotheses: total study time is equal; retention is judged after a delay.
  - ED.O.A3.3.2 Testing effect. Roediger and Karpicke (2006) found that recall practice beat rereading on a delayed test. On an immediate test the order reversed. Hypotheses: recall mostly succeeds or feedback follows; the test follows a delay.
  - ED.O.A3.3.3 Transfer of practice gains. Gains from spaced and recall practice are shown for the practised material. Transfer to other material or other domains is OPEN.
  - ED.O.A3.3.4 Interleaving. Mixing problem types in practice, rather than blocking them, raised later mathematics test scores in some studies (Rohrer and Taylor 2007). Its effect over a long course in ordinary classrooms is UNVERIFIED.
- ED.O.A3.4 Technology in teaching — **The learning effect of technology depends on the instructional use made of it, not on the device.** Effects depend on use, not presence.
  - ED.O.A3.4.1 Intelligent tutoring systems. VanLehn (2011) compared step-based tutoring systems with no tutoring and with human tutors. He reported effect sizes of the same order, about 0.76 and 0.79 (UNVERIFIED figures). Hypotheses: a stated domain; outcome tests in that domain. The software is CS (uses CS.O.A4).
  - ED.O.A3.4.2 Device provision. Giving devices to students, with no change in teaching, has shown small or no gains in test scores in several randomised evaluations. The pooled size is UNVERIFIED. The gain follows from use.
  - ED.O.A3.4.3 Multimedia design. Mayer's principles, such as removing extraneous material, reduce load in short laboratory lessons. Hypotheses: novice learners; short lessons; a transfer test. Their effect in long classroom courses is less tested.
  - ED.O.A3.4.4 Generative AI tutors. Whether chat-based language-model tutors raise learning more than other help is an OPEN programme. Trials are few, short and domain-specific (UNVERIFIED). The system is CS (uses CS.O.A4).
- ED.O.A3.5 Culturally sustaining pedagogy — **A pedagogy that claims to sustain a community's culture is a specified arrangement. Its effects on stated outcomes can be studied.** Kaupapa Māori education (kura kaupapa Māori) as an arrangement. The tradition-internal content is IK.
  - ED.O.A3.5.1 Culturally relevant pedagogy. Ladson-Billings (1995) states three aims: academic success, cultural competence and critical consciousness. A claim that a teacher practises it needs a description of the practice, not only the label.
  - ED.O.A3.5.2 Culturally sustaining pedagogy. Paris (2012) argued that teaching should sustain, and not only draw on, the language and cultural practice of a community. The aim is a stated position (W12 with W9). The empirical base for effects on attainment is thin (UNVERIFIED).
  - ED.O.A3.5.3 Bilingual and immersion programmes. In several syntheses, students taught partly in a home or heritage language attained in the majority language at least as well as peers in majority-language programmes (UNVERIFIED). Placement of Māori-medium education stays in ED.B.C6.

Named objects: cognitive load theory (hypotheses: limited working memory, schemas), worked-example effect, expertise-reversal effect, normalised gain (same instrument, class means), feedback-intervention theory, spacing effect (equal study time, delayed test), testing effect (delayed test, successful recall or feedback), interleaving, intelligent tutoring system, multimedia principles, culturally relevant pedagogy, culturally sustaining pedagogy.

### ED.O.A4 Assessment

**SCQA.** Situation. Learning cannot be seen directly. It is inferred from performance on instruments under stated conditions. Complication. One score is used for several purposes. Instruments carry error and variance unrelated to the construct, and the stakes attached to a score change behaviour. Question. Which claims about assessment does education own? Answer. Education owns the validity argument for a score interpretation and the evidence on score consistency. It also owns the use of assessment to change teaching and the effects of attaching stakes. The statistics are MA.O.A5. The choice to attach stakes is PO.

**Key line** (inductive; plural noun: assessment claims; dimension: what is asserted of the score or its use; order: meaning, then consistency, then use for teaching, then use for decisions). Members (4): Validity · Reliability · Formative assessment · High-stakes testing.

- ED.O.A4.1 Validity — **A score interpretation is valid to the extent that evidence supports the argument for its stated use.** An argument that score interpretations are supported (W9 with W4).
  - ED.O.A4.1.1 Unified validity. Messick (1989) treated validity as one judgement covering evidence and consequences of interpretation and use. The Standards (AERA, APA and NCME 2014) name five sources of evidence: content, response processes, internal structure, relations to other variables, and consequences. Hypothesis: the interpretation and use are stated first.
  - ED.O.A4.1.2 Argument-based validation. Kane states the chain of inferences from performance to score, to generalisation, to extrapolation, to decision. Validation checks the weakest link first. An inference with no evidence is a stated gap, not a pass.
  - ED.O.A4.1.3 Construct-irrelevant variance. A score can include variance unrelated to the construct, such as reading load in a mathematics word problem. It can also miss part of the construct. Either weakens validity. Differential item functioning (Mantel–Haenszel) assumes groups matched on a fair total score.
  - ED.O.A4.1.4 Predictive validity. A selection test's correlation with a later criterion, observed only on selected candidates, understates the population correlation. A correction needs assumptions about the selection. The criterion must be measured later and independently.
- ED.O.A4.2 Reliability — **Reliability describes score consistency over replications, and one test has no single reliability value.** Score consistency; the statistics are MA.O.A5 (psychometrics as bridge).
  - ED.O.A4.2.1 Classical test theory. An observed score is a true score plus error. Hypotheses: errors average zero; errors are unrelated to true scores; errors on separate testings are unrelated. The standard error of measurement follows from reliability. Spearman–Brown predicts reliability after a length change if added items are parallel (uses MA.O.A5).
  - ED.O.A4.2.2 Coefficient alpha. Cronbach (1951) gave a lower bound to reliability, equal to it only under essential tau-equivalence. Hypothesis: errors are uncorrelated. A high alpha does not show that one dimension underlies the items (uses MA.O.A5).
  - ED.O.A4.2.3 Item response theory. The chance of a response is modelled from a latent trait and item parameters. Hypotheses: one dimension underlies responses; responses are independent given the trait; the chance rises with the trait. The Rasch model also assumes equal discrimination (uses MA.O.A5).
  - ED.O.A4.2.4 Rater agreement. Scores from human raters need agreement checks. Cohen's kappa measures agreement beyond chance. Hypotheses: raters code independently; categories are exclusive; both code the same items. Rubric scoring assumes criteria are fixed in advance and a sample is double-scored.
- ED.O.A4.3 Formative assessment — **Assessment used to adjust teaching can raise attainment. The size of the effect is uncertain and depends on how the information is used.** Assessment used to change teaching.
  - ED.O.A4.3.1 Reported effect sizes. Black and Wiliam (1998) reviewed studies of formative assessment and reported effect sizes of about 0.4 to 0.7. Later reviews question the size and the breadth of the evidence. The size is CONTESTED.
  - ED.O.A4.3.2 The feedback loop. Assessment is formative only if it identifies a gap against a goal, someone acts on the gap, and the action is checked (Sadler 1989). Without the action the assessment is not formative.
  - ED.O.A4.3.3 Diagnostic items. An item whose wrong options map to specific misconceptions lets a teacher choose a response. Hypothesis: the options were validated by interviews or open-response data (uses ED.O.A2.1.2).
- ED.O.A4.4 High-stakes testing and accountability — **When a score drives rewards or sanctions, its meaning drifts from the construct it was built to measure.** Campbell's law: the more a quantitative indicator drives decisions, the more it is subject to corruption pressures (stated as a tendency).
  - ED.O.A4.4.1 Score inflation. Koretz and colleagues reported cases where gains on a high-stakes test exceeded gains on a parallel audit test of the same domain. Hypotheses: the audit test measures the same domain; the populations match. The size varies by system (UNVERIFIED).
  - ED.O.A4.4.2 Narrowing. Where tested subjects carry stakes, teacher surveys report more time given to them and less to untested subjects. The direction is reported in several systems. The size is UNVERIFIED. The policy decision to attach stakes is PO (uses PO.O.A4.4).
  - ED.O.A4.4.3 Value-added accountability. A value-added model compares actual with expected progress given prior attainment. Hypotheses: prior attainment on a comparable scale; students not sorted to teachers on unmeasured factors; enough students and years. Chetty, Friedman and Rockoff (2014) found little bias in their data. Rothstein (2010) found sorting bias in others. The bias is CONTESTED.
  - ED.O.A4.4.4 Standard setting. A cut score reflects judgement. The Angoff method sets it from judges' item-by-item estimates for a minimally competent candidate. Hypotheses: judges know the subject; judges share a picture of that candidate. Two qualified panels can set different cut scores.

Named objects: Messick's unified validity, five sources of evidence (Standards 2014), argument-based validation, construct-irrelevant variance, differential item functioning (matched groups, fair total score), classical test theory (hypotheses: errors average zero, are unrelated to true scores and to each other), Spearman–Brown formula (parallel items), coefficient alpha, item response theory (unidimensional, locally independent, monotone), Rasch model, Cohen's kappa, rubric scoring, formative assessment, Campbell's law, score inflation, value-added model, Angoff method.

### ED.O.A5 Institutions and systems

**SCQA.** Situation. Teaching happens in schools, systems and universities that set resources, rules and the mix of learners. Complication. Differences between schools mix a school's effect with who enrols. International comparisons mix systems with samples and cultures. Returns to schooling and legal duties are claimed by EC and LA. Question. Which claims about institutions does education own? Answer. Education owns identified effects of institutional features on learning, and the measured distribution of attainment across groups. Returns are EC, duties are LA and decisions are PO.

**Key line** (inductive; plural noun: institutional units; dimension: the unit of the institution the claim is about; order: the school, then the system, then tertiary study, then the distribution across groups). Members (4): Schools · School systems · Higher education · Equity and access.

- ED.O.A5.1 Schools — **Most variation in attainment lies within schools, and a school's measured effect depends on how intake is adjusted for.** School effects, leadership, class size.
  - ED.O.A5.1.1 Coleman Report. Coleman and colleagues (1966) found that, after family background, measured school resources explained a small share of attainment variance in their US sample. Hypotheses: resources are measured as listed; the design is cross-sectional. Its reading is CONTESTED. Between-school shares of 10 to 20 percent are often quoted (UNVERIFIED range).
  - ED.O.A5.1.2 Class size. Tennessee Project STAR (1985 to 1989) randomly assigned pupils and teachers to smaller or regular classes. Smaller classes raised early-grade test scores. The effect at scale and in later grades is CONTESTED.
  - ED.O.A5.1.3 Teacher effects. Teachers differ in measured effect on student progress, and the variation within a school exceeds the variation between schools in many datasets (UNVERIFIED). Shulman (1986) named pedagogical content knowledge, the knowledge of how to teach a topic. Its measured effect on progress is a separate claim.
  - ED.O.A5.1.4 Leadership and teacher learning. Robinson, Lloyd and Rowe (2008) pooled studies in a New Zealand synthesis. They reported larger effects for instructional leadership, about 0.42, than for transformational leadership, about 0.11 (UNVERIFIED figures). Timperley and colleagues (2007) synthesised teacher professional learning.
- ED.O.A5.2 School systems — **International assessments estimate system-level attainment within their sampling designs, and rankings carry sampling uncertainty.** International comparisons (PISA, TIMSS) are W4 within their sampling designs.
  - ED.O.A5.2.1 PISA. The OECD assesses 15-year-olds roughly every three years, since 2000, using a two-stage sample of schools then students. Scores are estimated as plausible values. Hypotheses: the sample frame covers the age group; items work comparably across countries.
  - ED.O.A5.2.2 TIMSS and PIRLS. The IEA has run TIMSS in grades 4 and 8 on a four-year cycle since 1995. It has run PIRLS in grade 4 on a five-year cycle since 2001. Both are linked to curricula. They test the intended and attained curriculum (ED.O.A1.2.1).
  - ED.O.A5.2.3 Rank uncertainty. A country's rank is an estimate with an interval. Small gaps between adjacent countries are not distinguishable. Hypothesis: sampling error follows the reported design.
  - ED.O.A5.2.4 Cause from comparison. That a policy of a high-scoring system causes its scores needs more than a cross-section (W5, not W4). The same holds for national reforms, such as the 1989 devolution of school governance to boards of trustees in New Zealand. Causal readings of such cases are CONTESTED.
- ED.O.A5.3 Higher education — **Student persistence in higher education depends on academic preparation and on integration into the institution, and the weights are disputed.** Universities, credentials.
  - ED.O.A5.3.1 Integration model of persistence. Tinto (1975) holds that persistence follows academic and social integration. Hypotheses: students enter with intentions and commitments; integration is measured before departure. It was built for residential colleges. Its fit for other students is CONTESTED.
  - ED.O.A5.3.2 Massification. Trow (1973) described elite, mass and universal systems, with thresholds of about 15 and 50 percent of the age group. The thresholds are conventions. The claim that institutional functions shift with participation is testable.
  - ED.O.A5.3.3 Learning gains in college. Arum and Roksa (2011) reported small gains in a generic skills test over the first two years in a US sample. The instrument and the sample are CONTESTED. Whether credentials raise productivity or only signal it is EC (uses EC.O.A2.3).
  - ED.O.A5.3.4 Student ratings of teaching. Student ratings are only weakly related to student learning on common examinations in some meta-analyses (Uttl and others 2017). The size of that relation is CONTESTED.
- ED.O.A5.4 Equity and access — **Attainment gaps between groups are measurable, and their causal decomposition needs identified designs.** Gaps by group. The returns to schooling are EC; the law on schooling is LA.
  - ED.O.A5.4.1 Measuring a gap. A gap is stated as a standardised mean difference on a common scale. Hypotheses: both groups take the same scale; the measure means the same in both; spreads are similar. The size depends on the scale chosen.
  - ED.O.A5.4.2 Summer learning loss. Cooper and colleagues (1996) pooled studies and reported lower scores after the summer break, more in mathematics. The claim that summer widens gaps by income is CONTESTED.
  - ED.O.A5.4.3 Early childhood programmes. Randomised programmes such as Perry Preschool reported test-score gains that faded, and benefits in adult outcomes. The adult return is EC. Whether large public programmes reproduce the results is CONTESTED.
  - ED.O.A5.4.4 Compulsory schooling. Natural experiments from changes in the school-leaving age report higher attainment for affected cohorts (UNVERIFIED across systems). The wage return to the extra schooling is EC. The legal duty to attend is LA.

Named objects: Coleman Report, intraclass correlation, Project STAR, pedagogical content knowledge, instructional leadership, PISA (hypotheses: sample frame, comparable items), TIMSS and PIRLS, plausible values, rank interval, Tinto's integration model, Trow's stages, summer learning loss, Perry Preschool, standardised mean difference (same scale, same meaning, similar spread).

4. The economic return is EC. The legal duty is LA. The political decision is PO.
5. MSC 97 is a bridge: the mathematics is MA; learning it is ED.
6. The learning line (Phase 2.1). A claim that learners changed under a stated arrangement (teaching, curriculum, assessment, institution) is ED. A claim about how learning or memory works in any mind, with no arrangement in it, is MS.O.A1.5. Test: delete the arrangement from the sentence. If the claim survives, it is MS; if it does not, it is ED. MS.O.A1.5.5 is retired and points here.
