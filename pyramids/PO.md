# PO — Politics

## 1. SCQA
**Situation.** Politics studies who holds power over a community, how they gain and keep it, and what they do with it.
**Complication.** v1 was master-level only. It mixed normative argument about legitimacy (PL) with empirical claims, and it misstated selectorate theory (the key quantity is the winning coalition).
**Question.** By what dimension can every empirical claim about political power be placed once?
**Answer.** By the aspect of power the claim concerns: its authority, its institutions, the behaviour of the governed, its policies, and its relations beyond the state.

## 2. Governing thought
Politics is exhausted by four faces of one pyramid: objects, methods, warrants and bridges.
Its objects divide into five aspects of power: authority, institutions, political behaviour, policy and administration, and relations among states.

### PO.O.A1 Authority
**SCQA.** Power is political when it claims the right to rule. Empirical politics can study whether people accept that claim, though not whether they should. What is claimed about authority? Key line: states and sovereignty, legitimacy as belief, regimes.

Situation. Every polity has some person or body that claims the right to command. Complication. The same words (state, legitimacy, democracy) carry a normative sense owned by PL and an empirical sense owned here. v1 used them in both senses within one sentence. Question. Which claims about the right to rule can be tested against observation? Answer. Claims about who holds the monopoly claim, about how far the governed accept it, and about how the regime is classified under stated rules.

Key line (inductive; plural noun: aspects of authority; dimension: what part of the claim to rule is observed; order: the claimant, then its acceptance, then its form). Members (3): States and sovereignty · Legitimacy as belief · Regimes.

- PO.O.A1.1 States and sovereignty — **A state is a community whose claim to a monopoly of legitimate force in a territory largely succeeds.** Weber's state: a community that successfully claims a monopoly of legitimate force in a territory.
  - PO.O.A1.1.1 State capacity is a matter of degree. Measures such as tax revenue as a share of output, census reach and control of territory rank states on a scale. A binary of state and failed state loses information.
  - PO.O.A1.1.2 Sovereignty has more than one component. Krasner separates domestic, interdependence, international legal and Westphalian sovereignty. A state can hold one component without another; recognition does not imply internal control.
  - PO.O.A1.1.3 War making built many states. Tilly's bellicist thesis holds that the cost of war drove European rulers to tax and to build administration. Its extension to post-colonial Africa and Latin America is CONTESTED.
- PO.O.A1.2 Legitimacy as belief — **Legitimacy in PO is the measured acceptance of a ruler's right, not its justification.** Measured acceptance of a ruler's right. Whether the rule is justified is PL.
  - PO.O.A1.2.1 Legitimacy is measured by indicators of belief and compliance. Survey items on trust and on the duty to obey, and rates of voluntary tax compliance, are the usual measures. Each measure states its population and wording.
  - PO.O.A1.2.2 Weber's three grounds classify claims to legitimacy as traditional, charismatic or legal-rational. The scheme is ideal-typical. A real regime usually mixes grounds, so the test is the dominant ground in a stated period.
  - PO.O.A1.2.3 Support has diffuse and specific forms. Easton separates diffuse support for the regime from specific support for current office-holders. The claim to test is that diffuse support falls less than specific support after poor performance.
- PO.O.A1.3 Regimes — **Regimes are classified by stated criteria, and different criteria give different counts.** Democracy, autocracy, hybrid regimes, classified by stated criteria (V-Dem, Polity: W12 for coding rules).
  - PO.O.A1.3.1 Minimal definitions classify a regime as democratic when office is filled by contested elections that incumbents can lose. Thicker definitions add civil liberties and rule of law. The share of democracies in a year depends on which definition is used.
  - PO.O.A1.3.2 Competitive authoritarian regimes hold real elections on an uneven field. Levitsky and Way define the type by abuse of state resources, media and law. Classification needs coding rules for when unevenness becomes decisive.
  - PO.O.A1.3.3 Income per head correlates with democracy across countries. Whether income causes transitions to democracy, or only makes existing democracies survive, is CONTESTED between endogenous and exogenous modernisation accounts.
  - PO.O.A1.3.4 Recent democratic erosion often proceeds by elected executives weakening checks, not by coups. The scale of erosion and its causes are OPEN; measured trends depend on the index and its expert coders.

Named objects: Weber's definition of the state; state capacity; Krasner's four sovereignties; bellicist thesis (hypotheses: interstate war is frequent, and rulers must extract resources to survive it); Weber's three types of legitimate rule (ideal types; no single real case is pure); diffuse and specific support; minimal and thick definitions of democracy; competitive authoritarianism; modernisation hypothesis (hypotheses: income is measured independently of regime; the direction of cause is identified); V-Dem and Polity indices (uses W12 for coding rules).

### PO.O.A2 Institutions
**SCQA.** Authority acts through rules and organisations. Which rules produce which outcomes? Key line: constitutions, electoral systems, parties, legislatures and executives.

Situation. Political actors work within constitutions, electoral laws, parties and assemblies. Complication. Institutions are chosen by actors who anticipate their effects. So a correlation between a rule and an outcome may run from the outcome to the rule. Question. Which effects of rules on outcomes can be stated with their conditions? Answer. Effects of constitutional form, of electoral formula, of party organisation, of veto structure and of coalition size, each stated with the conditions under which it holds.

Key line (inductive; plural noun: political institutions; dimension: which rule or organisation the claim is about; order: from the founding rules to the rules of office-holding). Members (5): Constitutional design · Electoral systems · Parties · Legislatures and executives · Selectorate theory.

- PO.O.A2.1 Constitutional design — **The political effect of a constitution's form is owned here; its legal text is LA.** Presidential and parliamentary systems, federalism. The legal text is LA; its political effect is PO (uses LA.O.A4.1).
  - PO.O.A2.1.1 Linz argued that presidential systems break down more often than parliamentary ones. Cheibub found the link largely explained by the military history of the countries that chose presidentialism. The claim is CONTESTED.
  - PO.O.A2.1.2 Federalism allows policy to differ across units. Whether it reduces or inflames conflict in ethnically divided states is CONTESTED; the result depends on whether unit borders follow group borders.
  - PO.O.A2.1.3 Judicial review constrains elected majorities only where courts are independent in practice. Measures of de facto independence and de jure independence diverge in many countries, so a claim must state which is used.
- PO.O.A2.2 Electoral systems — **Electoral formula and district size shape the number of parties.** Duverger's law: plurality rule in single-member districts tends toward two-party competition. It is a tendency with known exceptions (e.g. India, Canada).
  - PO.O.A2.2.1 Duverger's law works through two effects. The mechanical effect under-rewards third parties in seats. The psychological effect leads voters and elites to desert parties that cannot win.
  - PO.O.A2.2.2 The effective number of parties (Laakso–Taagepera) counts parties weighted by their shares. Cox's M+1 rule states that, with strategic voting, district competition tends toward at most M+1 viable candidates, where M is district magnitude.
  - PO.O.A2.2.3 Proportional formulas with larger district magnitude reduce the gap between vote shares and seat shares. Disproportionality is measured by an index such as Gallagher's least-squares index.
  - PO.O.A2.2.4 New Zealand moved from plurality to mixed-member proportional rule in 1996. The effective number of parliamentary parties rose after the change. This case is a test of the mechanical effect within one polity.
- PO.O.A2.3 Parties — **Party systems follow social cleavages, though less tightly than first claimed.** Party systems, organisation, cleavages.
  - PO.O.A2.3.1 Lipset and Rokkan held that European party systems reflected cleavages of centre–periphery, church–state, land–industry and owner–worker. Their freezing hypothesis, that these systems stayed stable for decades, is CONTESTED by later volatility.
  - PO.O.A2.3.2 The cartel party thesis of Katz and Mair holds that major parties draw closer to the state and to state funding. Its fit varies by country and is CONTESTED.
  - PO.O.A2.3.3 Party identification predicts vote choice better than most measured issue positions in panel studies. Whether it acts as a social identity or as a running tally of performance is CONTESTED.
- PO.O.A2.4 Legislatures and executives — **More veto players and greater distance between them make policy harder to change.** Veto players: policy stability rises with the number and ideological distance of veto players (Tsebelis).
  - PO.O.A2.4.1 In the veto-player model, an agenda setter can choose any point in the set that all veto players prefer to the status quo. The model predicts that agenda-setting power matters most when that set is large.
  - PO.O.A2.4.2 Riker's size principle predicts minimal winning coalitions in zero-sum settings. Oversized and minority cabinets are common in parliamentary systems, so the principle holds only under its stated conditions.
  - PO.O.A2.4.3 Mayhew found that divided government in the United States did not markedly reduce the passage of major laws. Later studies using other measures of output dispute the finding (CONTESTED).
- PO.O.A2.5 Selectorate theory — **Leaders choose private goods over public goods when the winning coalition is small relative to the selectorate.** Leaders keep office by rewarding a winning coalition W drawn from a selectorate S. When W is small relative to S, leaders favour private goods over public ones (Bueno de Mesquita et al., 2003).
  - PO.O.A2.5.1 The theory predicts that public goods provision rises with W. The prediction is tested against measures such as primary schooling and access to clean water.
  - PO.O.A2.5.2 The theory predicts that leaders with small W and large S survive longer in office, because coalition members fear exclusion from a new coalition. The mechanism is the loyalty norm.
  - PO.O.A2.5.3 Empirical tests often build W from regime-type indicators. Critics argue this makes W close to a democracy score, so its separate effect is CONTESTED.

Named objects: presidentialism and parliamentarism; federalism; judicial review (uses LA.O.A4.1); Duverger's law (hypotheses: single-member districts, plurality formula, strategic voters, a district-level tendency); mechanical and psychological effects; effective number of parties; M+1 rule (hypotheses: strategic voters with shared expectations of who is viable; short-term instrumental motives); Gallagher index; cleavage theory and freezing hypothesis; cartel party; party identification; veto player model (hypotheses: actors and ideal points can be identified; change needs every veto player's consent); size principle (hypotheses: zero-sum game, full information, rational players); selectorate theory (hypotheses: leaders seek to keep office; W and S can be measured apart from regime type; private and public goods can be told apart).

### PO.O.A3 Political behaviour
**SCQA.** The governed vote, protest, believe and organise. What explains their political action? Key line: voting, public opinion, participation and movements, conflict within states.

Situation. Citizens choose among parties, hold opinions, join or stay out of collective action, and sometimes take up arms. Complication. Formal theorems about voting are owned by mathematics, and the cognitive mechanism of opinion is owned by MS. The empirical regularities need a home that cites both. Question. Which claims about mass political action does PO own? Answer. The use of voting models, the measurement and effect of opinion, the causes of participation, and the onset of violent conflict within a state.

Key line (inductive; plural noun: forms of political action; dimension: what the governed do; order: from routine choice to violent challenge). Members (4): Voting · Public opinion · Participation and social movements · Conflict within states.

- PO.O.A3.1 Voting — **Under one dimension and single-peaked preferences, the median voter's ideal point wins under majority rule.** The median voter theorem: with one policy dimension, single-peaked preferences and majority rule, the median voter's ideal point beats any other. Theorem MA.B.C4; use PO.
  - PO.O.A3.1.1 Downs applied the theorem to two-party competition and predicted platform convergence on the median. Observed platforms diverge, so at least one hypothesis fails in practice (for example turnout, activists or entry).
  - PO.O.A3.1.2 With two or more dimensions, majority rule generally has no stable winner, and an agenda setter can reach many outcomes (McKelvey). The theorem is MA (uses MA.B.C4). Arrow's result on aggregation is EC.O.A5.5 (uses EC.O.A5.5).
  - PO.O.A3.1.3 Retrospective voting: incumbents tend to lose vote share when the economy performs poorly before an election. The size of the effect varies with the clarity of responsibility for policy.
  - PO.O.A3.1.4 The paradox of voting: an instrumental model, where the chance of being decisive is tiny, predicts very low turnout. Observed turnout is far higher, so the model needs added terms such as duty or social benefit.
- PO.O.A3.2 Public opinion — **Survey answers are samples from a person's considerations, shaped by question wording and elite cues.** Attitudes measured by survey. The cognitive mechanism is MS (uses MS.O.A1).
  - PO.O.A3.2.1 Zaller's receive–accept–sample model holds that answers reflect the considerations most accessible when asked. It predicts that the most politically aware are most responsive to elite cues from their own side.
  - PO.O.A3.2.2 The thermostatic model holds that public preference for more or less spending moves against the direction of recent policy. Tests use long series of spending preferences by domain.
  - PO.O.A3.2.3 Framing effects: the same policy described in different terms gets different levels of support in survey experiments. Effects are often smaller when respondents hear competing frames.
- PO.O.A3.3 Participation and social movements — **Collective action is under-supplied in large groups unless something changes individual incentives.** Turnout, collective action. Olson: large groups under-provide collective goods without selective incentives (uses EC.O.A5.3).
  - PO.O.A3.3.1 Olson's claim predicts that durable large groups offer selective incentives or coercion. Counter-cases are taken as tests of whether social or expressive benefits act as selective incentives.
  - PO.O.A3.3.2 The resource model holds that time, money and civic skills predict who participates. Education is the strongest single correlate of participation in many surveys.
  - PO.O.A3.3.3 Field experiments find that social pressure, such as telling households that their turnout record may be shared, raises turnout. Effect sizes are larger for mailed pressure than for neutral reminders.
  - PO.O.A3.3.4 Chenoweth and Stephan report that nonviolent campaigns against regimes succeeded more often than violent ones in their dataset. The result and its causes are CONTESTED, partly on case selection.
- PO.O.A3.4 Conflict within states — **The onset of civil war, coups and revolutions is PO; the fighting is ST.** Civil war onset, coups, revolution. The conduct of the fighting is ST.
  - PO.O.A3.4.1 Fearon and Laitin found that low income per head, large population and rough terrain predicted civil war onset better than ethnic diversity. Whether low income measures weak state capacity or grievance is CONTESTED.
  - PO.O.A3.4.2 Hybrid regimes appear more prone to civil war than full democracies or full autocracies. Part of the finding may be an artefact, because some Polity components code political violence (CONTESTED).
  - PO.O.A3.4.3 Coup-proofing: rulers who divide armed forces into rival units face fewer successful coups. The claimed cost is lower military effectiveness against external foes (UNVERIFIED as a general regularity).

Named objects: median voter theorem (uses MA.B.C4; hypotheses: one dimension, single-peaked preferences, majority rule, sincere voting); Downsian convergence (hypotheses: two parties, full turnout, office-seeking parties, no entry); McKelvey's chaos result (uses MA.B.C4); economic voting; paradox of voting; receive–accept–sample model (hypotheses: considerations are sampled by accessibility; awareness is measured); thermostatic model; framing effect; Olson's logic of collective action (uses EC.O.A5.3; hypotheses: rational individuals, a non-excludable good, a large group); resource model of participation; social pressure turnout effect; civil war onset models.

### PO.O.A4 Policy and administration
**SCQA.** Governments make and carry out policy. How is policy made and delivered? Key line: agenda and decision, bureaucracy, implementation, policy effects.

Situation. Policy passes from attention to decision, then to agencies, then to delivery. Complication. Much of the literature consists of frameworks that organise cases but state no falsifiable prediction. Policy effects are claimed by the fields whose outcomes change. Question. Which claims about the making and delivery of policy are testable, and which belong to PO? Answer. Claims about the distribution of policy change, the control of agencies, the failure points of delivery, and the political causes of policy.

Key line (inductive; plural noun: policy stages; dimension: the stage of the policy process; order: from attention to consequence). Members (4): Agenda and decision · Bureaucracy · Implementation · Policy effects.

- PO.O.A4.1 Agenda and decision — **Policy change comes in long stable periods broken by rare large shifts.** Punctuated equilibrium; the multiple streams model (a framework, W9).
  - PO.O.A4.1.1 Punctuated equilibrium predicts that the distribution of annual budget changes is leptokurtic: many small changes, few medium ones, and more large ones than a normal curve. Baumgartner and Jones report this across several countries.
  - PO.O.A4.1.2 The multiple streams framework holds that change happens when problem, policy and political streams join in a window. It organises cases but makes few predictions that could fail, so it is held as a framework (W9).
  - PO.O.A4.1.3 Lindblom's incrementalism holds that decisions proceed by small adjustments to existing policy. It predicts small year-on-year change and is challenged by the heavy tails in A4.1.1.
- PO.O.A4.2 Bureaucracy — **Delegation to agencies creates drift, and legislators design controls to limit it.** Delegation, principal–agent control (theory EC.O.A2.3).
  - PO.O.A4.2.1 Agencies drift from the wishes of the legislature when they hold private information and their preferences differ. The model is EC (uses EC.O.A2.3); its use on agencies is PO.
  - PO.O.A4.2.2 McCubbins and Schwartz distinguish police-patrol oversight, which is direct, from fire-alarm oversight, which relies on complaints by affected groups. They predict that legislators prefer fire alarms because they cost less.
  - PO.O.A4.2.3 Evans and Rauch report that merit recruitment and career paths in core agencies are associated with faster growth. The causal direction is CONTESTED.
- PO.O.A4.3 Implementation — **The more steps a decision must pass, the more likely delivery falls short.** The gap between decision and delivery.
  - PO.O.A4.3.1 Pressman and Wildavsky showed that if each of many clearance points has a high chance of agreement, the joint chance can still be low. The claim is arithmetic once clearances are independent.
  - PO.O.A4.3.2 Lipsky's street-level bureaucrats make policy in practice by using discretion under heavy caseloads. The claim predicts variation in delivery across front-line staff applying the same rule.
- PO.O.A4.4 Policy effects — **PO owns the political cause of a policy; the field of the outcome owns its effect.** The causal effect of a policy is owned by the field of the outcome (EC, MD, ED), and PO cites it. The political cause of the policy is PO.
  - PO.O.A4.4.1 Partisan theory predicts that the party in office changes policy in the direction of its supporters. Tests compare policy before and after close elections, where the winner is near random.
  - PO.O.A4.4.2 Policy feedback: a policy, once in place, creates groups that defend it. The claim predicts that programmes with broad visible benefits are harder to cut.
  - PO.O.A4.4.3 Policy diffusion: governments adopt policies earlier when neighbours or peers have adopted them. Separating learning from competition and imitation is the open test.

Named objects: punctuated equilibrium (hypotheses: limited attention, friction in institutions); multiple streams framework (W9; not a testable law); incrementalism; principal–agent model (uses EC.O.A2.3; hypotheses: the agent has private information, preferences differ, contracts are incomplete); police-patrol and fire-alarm oversight; Weberian bureaucracy; implementation clearance arithmetic (hypotheses: clearances are independent and all required); street-level bureaucracy; partisan theory; policy feedback; policy diffusion.

### PO.O.A5 Relations among states
**SCQA.** States act in a system without a common government. What patterns hold among them? Key line: international order, cooperation and institutions, war and peace.

Situation. States interact with no authority above them able to enforce agreements. Complication. Large paradigms (realism, liberalism, constructivism) are often presented as rival laws. Strategy claims about coercion are owned by ST. Question. Which claims about the pattern of relations among states can be tested? Answer. Claims about the distribution of power, about when cooperation lasts, and about when war breaks out or does not.

Key line (inductive; plural noun: patterns among states; dimension: the kind of outcome in the system; order: structure, then cooperation, then violence). Members (3): International order · Cooperation and international institutions · War and peace.

- PO.O.A5.1 International order — **The distribution of power among states is claimed to shape stability, but the main programmes disagree on how.** Anarchy, balance of power, hegemony. Realist, liberal and constructivist claims are competing programmes (CONTESTED).
  - PO.O.A5.1.1 Balance-of-power theory predicts that states balance against a rising power more often than they join it. Evidence on balancing against bandwagoning varies by region and period (CONTESTED).
  - PO.O.A5.1.2 Hegemonic stability theory holds that an open economic order needs a dominant state to supply it. Critics note open orders that outlasted the decline of the leading state (CONTESTED).
  - PO.O.A5.1.3 Power transition theory holds that war is most likely when a challenger nears parity with a dominant state and is dissatisfied with the order. Tests depend on how parity and dissatisfaction are measured.
- PO.O.A5.2 Cooperation and international institutions — **International institutions can sustain cooperation by lowering the cost of agreement and monitoring.** Regimes, alliances, the UN. The treaty's legal force is LA (uses LA.O.A1.4).
  - PO.O.A5.2.1 Keohane's institutionalism holds that regimes reduce transaction costs and supply information among states. The economic theory of transaction costs is EC (uses EC.O.A3.3).
  - PO.O.A5.2.2 Repeated interaction with a long shadow of the future can sustain cooperation without enforcement. The game-theoretic result is EC (uses EC.O.A3.5); its use for states is PO.
  - PO.O.A5.2.3 Alliance commitments are honoured in a majority of cases where they are invoked, on one coding of alliance treaties (UNVERIFIED as to the exact share). Reliability falls when the allies' interests have changed since signing.
- PO.O.A5.3 War and peace — **Pairs of democracies rarely fight each other, and war usually needs some obstacle to a bargain.** The democratic peace: pairs of democracies rarely fight each other. It is an empirical regularity; its causes are CONTESTED. The strategy of coercion and conduct is ST.
  - PO.O.A5.3.1 The democratic peace is dyadic. Democracies as a group are not markedly less war-prone than other states; the regularity holds for pairs.
  - PO.O.A5.3.2 Fearon's bargaining model shows that, since war is costly, a settlement both sides prefer exists. War then needs private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, or indivisible stakes. Deterrence and the security dilemma are ST (uses ST.O.A3.1, ST.O.A3.4).
  - PO.O.A5.3.3 Audience costs: leaders who make public threats and back down suffer at home, which makes threats more credible. Survey experiments support the domestic reaction; whether it shapes crises in practice is CONTESTED.
  - PO.O.A5.3.4 The capitalist peace holds that economic openness or development, not democracy, explains the low rate of war between some pairs. It is a rival to A5.3.1 and is CONTESTED.

Named objects: anarchy; balance-of-power theory; hegemonic stability theory; power transition theory; neoliberal institutionalism (uses EC.O.A3.3); cooperation under repeated play (uses EC.O.A3.5; hypotheses: indefinite horizon, patient players, observable defection); democratic peace (empirical regularity; hypotheses: stated definitions of democracy and war, dyad-year data); bargaining model of war (hypotheses: rational unitary actors, war is costly, divisible stakes); audience costs; capitalist peace.

## 8. Assignment rules
1. Place an empirical claim by the aspect of power.
2. Whether rule is justified is PL; whether people believe it is justified is PO.
3. Legal validity is LA; the political cause or effect of a law is PO.
4. The policy's causal effect belongs to its outcome field; its political origin is PO.
5. The conduct of war, coercion and the choice of game are ST; war onset and the international order are PO.
6. The theorem is MA; its political use is PO.
7. A dated political event is HI; its explanation is PO.
