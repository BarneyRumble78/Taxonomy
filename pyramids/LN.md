# LN — Language

Standard v2 cited, not copied. Built up from prototype_history_language.md; its five members are kept.

## 1. SCQA
**Situation.** Linguistics states how human languages are produced, built, understood, varied and changed. It is taught by levels: phonetics through pragmatics, plus sociolinguistics and historical linguistics.
**Complication.** v1 had no home for language. PL owned meaning as a concept, MS owned processing and CS owned language models. No pyramid owned the grammar of a language, its sound system or its history.
**Question.** What partition places every claim about language once, with stated borders to PL, MS, CS and AR?
**Answer.** Place a claim by the aspect of language it is about: signal, form, meaning, variation in use, or diversity.

## 2. Governing thought
Language is exhausted by four faces of one pyramid: objects, methods, warrants and bridges.
Its objects divide into five aspects of language: signal, form, meaning, variation in use, and diversity.

## 3. Key line
Dimension: **the aspect of language the sentence is about.** Order: from the signal to the family of languages.
Key line type: inductive. Plural noun: aspects of language. Members: five.
1. Signal
2. Form
3. Meaning
4. Variation in use
5. Diversity

A particular language is a catalogue entry on the same five cells. There is no "exotic languages" bucket.

## 4. Face O — Objects

Each A-cell below states a fuller SCQA, a key line with its member count, and a list of named objects. Each sub-cell opens with a bold claim and has four second-level cells, written one level down. A method or model is named together with its hypotheses. Where another field owns a theorem or an object, the cell says "uses" and gives the owner. Mathematics owns every theorem.

### LN.O.A1 Signal
**SCQA.** **Situation.** Language reaches other people as a signal: sound in speech, movement in sign and marks in writing. **Complication.** One signal can be described by its physics, by the contrasts it carries or by the convention that records it. v1 let PH, MS and CS claim acoustics, perception and speech technology. **Question.** Which claims about the signal does linguistics own? **Answer.** LN owns the articulatory, acoustic and perceptual description of speech. It also owns the sound and sign categories of a language and the conventions that record language in writing. PH owns the physics of sound waves, MS owns real-time processing and CS owns speech technology.

**Key line** (inductive; plural noun: descriptions of the signal; dimension: the form in which the signal is described; order: physical form, then spoken categories, then signed categories, then written record). Members (4): Phonetics · Phonology · Sign phonology · Writing systems. The earlier key line named three members and omitted writing systems, which was already a sub-cell.

- **LN.O.A1.1 Phonetics.** **Phonetics describes how speech sounds are produced, transmitted and heard, in measurable terms.** Articulation, acoustics and perception are its three descriptions. Source–filter theory of speech (Fant, 1960) cites PH acoustics.
  - LN.O.A1.1.1 Articulatory classes. Pulmonic consonants are classed by place, manner and voicing, and vowels by height, backness and rounding. The classes are conventions (W12) that summarise gestures. They do not claim fixed articulator positions.
  - LN.O.A1.1.2 Source–filter model. Voiced speech is a glottal source shaped by vocal-tract resonances called formants. Hypotheses: source and tract act independently, the tract is linear and time-invariant, and sound travels as a plane wave. Nasal sounds and strong source–tract interaction break the fit. The wave physics is PH (uses PH).
  - LN.O.A1.1.3 Acoustic correlates. The first two formants, F1 and F2, separate vowel qualities. F1 varies inversely with tongue height and F2 varies with backness. Voice onset time separates voiced from voiceless stops in many languages (Lisker and Abramson, 1964). Category boundaries differ by language.
  - LN.O.A1.1.4 Speech perception. Listeners identify stops along a voice-onset-time continuum with a sharp boundary, and discrimination peaks near it. Discrimination within a category stays above chance, so perception is only partly categorical. Infants lose sensitivity to non-native contrasts during the first year (Werker and Tees, 1984). The perceptual process is MS (uses MS.O.A1.1).
- **LN.O.A1.2 Phonology.** **Phonology states the contrasts of a language's sound system and the patterns that relate them.** The phoneme and the minimal pair; distinctive features (Chomsky and Halle, *The Sound Pattern of English*, 1968); Optimality Theory (Prince and Smolensky, 1993).
  - LN.O.A1.2.1 Phoneme and allophone. A phoneme is a contrastive unit shown by minimal pairs in one variety. Allophones are variants whose distribution is predictable from context. Whether the phoneme is a psychological unit or only an analytic one is CONTESTED.
  - LN.O.A1.2.2 Distinctive features. A small set of features defines the segments of a language and groups sounds that pattern together in rules. Hypothesis: features are drawn from a universal set. Whether features are innate or emerge from the patterns of each language is CONTESTED.
  - LN.O.A1.2.3 Optimality Theory. A grammar ranks violable constraints, and the output is the candidate that violates the highest-ranked constraints least. Hypotheses: constraints are universal, rankings differ by language, candidates are freely generated and evaluation is parallel. Every ranking should then be a possible language. Serial alternatives are CONTESTED.
  - LN.O.A1.2.4 Syllable, stress and tone. Consonants in an onset tend to rise in sonority toward the vowel, with exceptions such as English *street*. Stress and tone are patterns over syllables. A poem's metre is AR.O.A2.1, and the stress system it exploits is here (uses AR.O.A2.1).
- **LN.O.A1.3 Sign phonology.** **Sign languages have a phonology: a finite set of meaningless formational units combines to make signs.** Handshape, location and movement as parameters (Stokoe, 1960). Sign languages are full natural languages.
  - LN.O.A1.3.1 Parameters. A sign is described by handshape, location and movement, with orientation and non-manual features added in later models. Minimal pairs that differ in one parameter are reported in described sign languages. The test is the minimal pair test.
  - LN.O.A1.3.2 Duality of patterning. Signs are built from units that carry no meaning, as words are built from phonemes. Stokoe (1960) argued this for American Sign Language. Hypothesis: the formational units contrast independently of the meaning of the sign.
  - LN.O.A1.3.3 Modality effects. The hands can realise several parameters at once, so sign phonology shows more simultaneity than speech. Iconic forms are more frequent in sign lexicons. They coexist with conventional, language-specific forms (UNVERIFIED as to cross-linguistic frequency).
  - LN.O.A1.3.4 Independent sign languages. Each sign language is a language of its own, with a grammar that differs from that of the spoken language around it. New Zealand Sign Language became an official language of New Zealand in 2006.
- **LN.O.A1.4 Writing systems.** **A writing system is a convention that records language, and systems differ in the unit of language they record.** The IPA is a convention (W12); orthography is not sound.
  - LN.O.A1.4.1 Script types. Scripts are classed by the unit they map: phonemes (alphabets), consonants (abjads), consonant–vowel units (abugidas), syllables (syllabaries) or morphemes (logographies). Most scripts mix types. The classes are descriptive conventions (W12).
  - LN.O.A1.4.2 Orthographic depth. Spelling–sound correspondence is regular in Finnish and Spanish and irregular in English. Reading acquisition is reported to be slower in deep orthographies (UNVERIFIED as to effect size). The reading process is MS (uses MS.O.A1.5.3).
  - LN.O.A1.4.3 Phonetic notation. The IPA is a stipulated set of symbols, revised by decision of the International Phonetic Association (W12). A transcription is an analysis. A broad transcription records contrasts and a narrow one records phonetic detail.
  - LN.O.A1.4.4 Writing is secondary. Most languages have no writing system. Writing first appears in the record around 3300 BCE (UNVERIFIED as to date). Spelling reform and standard orthography are decisions (W12), not findings about sound.

Named objects: formant; voice onset time; place, manner and voicing; source–filter model (independent source and tract, linear tract, plane waves); minimal pair test (words differ in one sound in the same position, one variety); phoneme; distinctive feature (universal set, CONTESTED); Optimality Theory (universal violable constraints, language-particular ranking, parallel evaluation); sonority sequencing; Stokoe's parameters; International Phonetic Alphabet.

### LN.O.A2 Form
**SCQA.** **Situation.** Words and sentences are built from smaller units by rules that speakers follow without being taught. **Complication.** Descriptions differ by framework, and a framework's rules are often reported as facts about the language. The formal class of a grammar belongs to MA, and the speaker's processing belongs to MS. **Question.** Which claims about the build of words and sentences does linguistics own? **Answer.** LN owns the units of a language and the rules or constraints that combine them. It also owns the formal class assigned to the resulting strings.

**Key line** (inductive; plural noun: levels of structure; dimension: the unit the claim is about; order: parts of a word, then combinations of words, then stored items, then formal class). Members (4): Morphology · Syntax · The lexicon · Formal grammars. The earlier key line named three members and omitted formal grammars, which was already a sub-cell.

- **LN.O.A2.1 Morphology.** **Morphology states how words are built from morphemes and how the forms of one word differ.** Morphemes; inflection versus derivation.
  - LN.O.A2.1.1 Morpheme and exponence. A morpheme is a smallest pairing of form and meaning or function. A one-to-one mapping from morph to morpheme fails in root-and-pattern morphology, reduplication and cumulative exponence. Morpheme-based and word-and-paradigm accounts are rivals (CONTESTED).
  - LN.O.A2.1.2 Inflection and derivation. Inflection gives the forms of one lexeme and derivation makes a new lexeme. The line is gradient, and its diagnostics are category change, productivity and distance from the root. Greenberg's Universal 28 states, as a tendency, that derivational affixes lie closer to the root than inflectional ones.
  - LN.O.A2.1.3 Productivity. Speakers apply inflection rules to new words, as in the wug test with English plurals (Berko, 1958). Whether the rule is a symbolic rule or a pattern in a network is CONTESTED. The process is MS (uses MS.O.A1.5.3).
  - LN.O.A2.1.4 Degree of synthesis. Languages vary in the number of morphemes per word. Isolating, agglutinating, fusional and polysynthetic are tendencies along an index of synthesis, not exclusive classes. A language is placed by counting morphemes per word in a text sample.
- **LN.O.A2.2 Syntax.** **Syntax states which strings of words are sentences of a language and what structure they have.** Constituency and recursion; dependency versus phrase structure; agreement.
  - LN.O.A2.2.1 Constituency tests. A word group is a constituent if it can be replaced by a pro-form, moved, coordinated or given as a fragment answer. Hypotheses: acceptability judgements are reliable and several tests agree. Where the tests disagree, the constituent claim is weak.
  - LN.O.A2.2.2 Phrase structure and dependency. Both represent relations between words. Projective dependency grammars and context-free phrase structure grammars generate the same sets of strings. That result is a theorem, owned by MA (uses MA.O.A1; UNVERIFIED as to source). The choice between the two rests on coverage of data such as free word order.
  - LN.O.A2.2.3 Recursion. A phrase can contain a phrase of the same category. Grammars allow unbounded embedding, while processing limits keep centre-embedding to a few levels in practice. Whether every language shows embedding is CONTESTED, and the answer depends on whether recursion means the mechanism or the observed pattern.
  - LN.O.A2.2.4 Alignment and agreement. Agreement is covariation of forms between words. Languages mark the subject of an intransitive verb like the agent (nominative–accusative) or like the patient (ergative–absolutive), as in Basque. Many ergative systems are split by tense, aspect or person.
- **LN.O.A2.3 The lexicon.** **The lexicon is the stock of stored items, and each item carries information about how it combines.** Word classes, argument structure.
  - LN.O.A2.3.1 Word classes. Noun and verb are the best candidates for categories found in every language. Whether every language distinguishes them is CONTESTED, with claims for some Salish languages. A class is defined by distribution, not by meaning.
  - LN.O.A2.3.2 Argument structure. A verb selects a number and kind of arguments. Verbs that share meaning components share alternation patterns, such as the English dative alternation (Levin, 1993). The link from meaning to syntactic behaviour holds in part.
  - LN.O.A2.3.3 Frequency laws. Word frequency falls with rank roughly as rank to the power minus one (Zipf's law). Vocabulary grows as a power of text length (Heaps' law). Both are empirical fits, not theorems, and the fit depends on corpus size and tokenisation. The distributions as mathematics are MA.O.A5 (uses MA.O.A5).
  - LN.O.A2.3.4 Idioms and constructions. Expressions whose meaning is not computed from their parts, such as *kick the bucket*, must be listed. Construction grammar treats rules and stored items as one kind of form–meaning pair. Where the line between rule and list falls is CONTESTED.
- **LN.O.A2.4 Formal grammars.** **Natural-language phenomena are classed by the formal grammar needed to generate them, and the classes belong to MA.** The Chomsky hierarchy is MA.O.A1; LN uses it to class natural-language phenomena. Whether natural languages are mildly context-sensitive is CONTESTED.
  - LN.O.A2.4.1 Non-regularity. A finite-state grammar cannot generate unbounded centre-embedding. As an idealised competence, English is therefore not a regular language. Hypothesis: memory limits are idealised away (uses MA.O.A1).
  - LN.O.A2.4.2 Cross-serial dependencies. Swiss German subordinate clauses with case-marked objects in cross-serial order are argued to lie beyond context-free power (Shieber, 1985). Hypotheses: the string set is intersected with a regular language, and the case marking is required for grammaticality. The Dutch case concerns structure, not strings (uses MA.O.A1).
  - LN.O.A2.4.3 Mild context-sensitivity. Tree-adjoining grammars generate cross-serial patterns and can be parsed in polynomial time. Mild context-sensitivity as a bound on natural language (Joshi, 1985) is CONTESTED. Counterexamples have been proposed, and each is disputed.
  - LN.O.A2.4.4 Probabilistic grammars. A weighted grammar assigns probabilities to structures, and its fit is tested on held-out corpus data. Hypotheses: the grammar class is stated and the training corpus is representative. Language-model outputs are CS (uses CS.O.A4.4). Whether they show a grammatical generalisation is tested here, on minimal pairs of grammatical and ungrammatical sentences.

Named objects: morpheme; lexeme; paradigm; wug test (novel word, regular rule applied by speakers); constituency tests (judgements reliable, tests agree); phrase structure grammar; dependency grammar; valency; Zipf's law and Heaps' law (large corpus, fixed tokenisation); tree-adjoining grammar (polynomial parsing); probabilistic grammar (representative corpus); Chomsky hierarchy (uses MA.O.A1).

### LN.O.A3 Meaning
**SCQA.** **Situation.** Expressions mean something alone and in context, and hearers recover more than the sentence says. **Complication.** Meaning as a concept is PL's, processing is MS's and the sense of a poem is AR's. The border between what a sentence means and what a speaker conveys is itself disputed. **Question.** Which claims about meaning in a language does linguistics own? **Answer.** LN owns the claims tested by truth-value and felicity judgements and by usage data: word sense, composition, contextual inference and meaning built into grammar.

**Key line** (inductive; plural noun: layers of meaning; dimension: what carries the meaning; order: the word, then the sentence, then the context of use, then the grammatical category). Members (4): Lexical semantics · Compositional semantics · Pragmatics · Grammaticalised meaning. The earlier key line named three members and omitted grammaticalised meaning, which was already a sub-cell.

- **LN.O.A3.1 Lexical semantics.** **Lexical semantics states what words mean through their relations to other words and to uses.** Sense relations; Saussure's arbitrariness of the sign (*Cours*, 1916).
  - LN.O.A3.1.1 Sense relations. Synonymy, antonymy, hyponymy and meronymy are tested by entailment. Hyponymy is transitive, and exact synonymy is rare. Antonyms divide into gradable pairs, such as *hot* and *cold*, and complementary pairs, such as *dead* and *alive*.
  - LN.O.A3.1.2 Arbitrariness. The link between a word form and its meaning is conventional (Saussure, 1916). Onomatopoeia and sound symbolism are exceptions, and ideophones show systematic form–meaning links. Most of the lexicon is conventional, but the proportion is UNVERIFIED.
  - LN.O.A3.1.3 Polysemy and homonymy. Polysemy is one form with related senses, and homonymy is one form with unrelated senses. Etymology and speaker judgement can give different answers. Neither criterion alone settles a case.
  - LN.O.A3.1.4 Colour terms and categories. Basic colour terms are claimed to be added in a fixed order across languages (Berlin and Kay, 1969). The strength of that order is CONTESTED. Weak effects of colour vocabulary on discrimination speed are reported, and their size is CONTESTED. The strong claim that language determines thought is not supported by those results. The behavioural effect is MS (uses MS.O.A1.1).
- **LN.O.A3.2 Compositional semantics.** **The meaning of a complex expression is a function of the meanings of its parts and how they combine, in a stated fragment.** Frege's principle (concept cited from PL); Montague grammar.
  - LN.O.A3.2.1 Compositionality as a hypothesis. The principle holds for a fragment when the syntax and the meaning assignment are fixed in advance. Idioms and context-sensitive expressions are problem cases. Without a limit on the allowed functions the principle is nearly empty. The concept is PL.O.A3.1 (uses PL.O.A3.1).
  - LN.O.A3.2.2 Montague grammar. A fragment of English is given a model-theoretic semantics by translation into typed intensional logic (Montague, 1970 and 1973). Hypotheses: syntax and semantics are homomorphic, and truth is relative to a model and an index. The logic is MA's (uses MA.O.A1).
  - LN.O.A3.2.3 Generalised quantifiers. Determiners such as *every* and *most* denote relations between sets (Barwise and Cooper, 1981). The non-definability of *most* in first-order logic is a theorem, owned by MA (uses MA.O.A1). Sentences such as *Every student read a book* have two scope readings, tested by judgement.
  - LN.O.A3.2.4 Entailment and polarity. A sentence entails another if the second is true whenever the first is true, and speakers' entailment judgements are the data. Negative polarity items such as *any* occur in downward-entailing contexts. The account is CONTESTED in full generality, since some licensing contexts are not downward entailing.
- **LN.O.A3.3 Pragmatics.** **Pragmatics states what a hearer infers from an utterance, beyond its sentence meaning, given the context.** Grice's cooperative principle and implicature (concept PL, empirical patterns LN); presupposition; deixis; politeness.
  - LN.O.A3.3.1 Implicature. A conversational implicature is calculable from the maxims, cancellable and not detachable. *Some* implicates *not all*, and *some, indeed all* cancels the inference. Relevance theory (Sperber and Wilson, 1986) is a rival account (CONTESTED). The concept is PL.O.A3.1 (uses PL.O.A3.1).
  - LN.O.A3.3.2 Presupposition. A presupposition survives negation and questioning. Triggers include definite descriptions, factive verbs and *stop*. How presuppositions project from embedded clauses is CONTESTED between accounts.
  - LN.O.A3.3.3 Deixis. Expressions such as *I*, *here* and *now* take their reference from speaker, place and time. Demonstrative systems have two terms in English and three in languages such as Spanish and Japanese. The referent is found from the context of utterance.
  - LN.O.A3.3.4 Speech acts and politeness. Austin's performatives (1962) show that utterances do things as well as describe. Brown and Levinson (1987) explain politeness as the management of face-threatening acts. Its claimed universality is CONTESTED by cross-cultural work.
- **LN.O.A3.4 Grammaticalised meaning.** **Meaning that a language encodes in its grammar is a language-particular claim, and languages differ in what they encode.** E.g. te reo Māori pronouns distinguish dual from plural, and inclusive from exclusive.
  - LN.O.A3.4.1 Person and number. Pronoun systems differ in the distinctions they encode, including dual number and the inclusive–exclusive contrast in *we*. In typological samples a substantial minority of languages show the inclusive–exclusive contrast (UNVERIFIED as to proportion).
  - LN.O.A3.4.2 Tense, aspect and mood. Languages differ in which time relations are obligatory in grammar. Mandarin has no obligatory tense marking and relies on aspect and context. The Whorfian claim that a language without tense has no concept of time has not been supported by fieldwork.
  - LN.O.A3.4.3 Evidentiality. Some languages mark the source of information, such as seen, heard or reported, as an obligatory category, as in Turkish (Aikhenvald, 2004). Whether evidential and epistemic marking are separate categories is CONTESTED.
  - LN.O.A3.4.4 Spatial reference. Languages differ in the frame they prefer for locating objects: relative, intrinsic or absolute (Levinson, 2003). Some use cardinal directions in everyday speech. Whether this shapes non-linguistic spatial memory is CONTESTED. The cognitive effect is MS (uses MS).

Named objects: sense relation; entailment (truth in all models where the premise is true); compositionality (fixed syntax and meaning assignment); Montague grammar (typed intensional logic, syntax–semantics homomorphism); generalised quantifier; conversational implicature (cooperative speakers, shared context); presupposition projection; deixis; speech act; politeness theory (face threat, CONTESTED universality); evidentiality; basic colour term hierarchy (CONTESTED).

### LN.O.A4 Variation in use
**SCQA.** **Situation.** No speaker uses one fixed variety. The choice of variant tracks age, class, region, setting and audience. **Complication.** Some frameworks report variation as rules and others as noise. Language and dialect are separated by social and political criteria as much as by structure. Social structure is MS's and language policy is PO's. **Question.** Which claims about variation in use does linguistics own? **Answer.** LN owns patterned variation within and between speech communities, the outcomes of contact and the vitality of languages. Policy and social structure are cited from their owners (uses PO, MS).

**Key line** (inductive; plural noun: patterns of variation; dimension: what the variation tracks; order: the speaker's social position, then the situation, then contact between languages, then the survival of a language). Members (4): Social variation · Register and genre · Contact and multilingualism · Endangerment and revitalisation.

- **LN.O.A4.1 Social variation.** **Linguistic variants correlate with social categories, and the pattern of correlation is the object of study.** The sociolinguistic variable (Labov, 1966).
  - LN.O.A4.1.1 The variable. A sociolinguistic variable is two or more ways of saying the same thing, with variant frequencies counted by group. Accountability requires counting every place where the variant could occur, including non-uses. Sameness of meaning is hard to show for syntax and discourse (CONTESTED).
  - LN.O.A4.1.2 Stratification. In studied urban communities, use of the prestige variant rises with social class and with formality of style. The lower middle class often shows the steepest style shift and may overshoot the highest class in the most formal style (Labov, 1966). The pattern is not found in every community.
  - LN.O.A4.1.3 Apparent time. Age differences within a community are read as change in progress. Hypotheses: speech is stable after adolescence, and the sample is stratified by age. Age-grading, as in slang, defeats the inference. Panel and trend studies test it.
  - LN.O.A4.1.4 Gender, age and ethnicity. In many studied communities women use more prestige variants in stable variables and lead in changes that carry no stigma. The generalisation is CONTESTED across communities. A correlation is not a causal account, which needs W5.
- **LN.O.A4.2 Register and genre.** **Speakers shift variety with the situation, and the shift is patterned.** Situational variation; diglossia (Ferguson, 1959).
  - LN.O.A4.2.1 Diglossia. A high variety is used in writing and formal speech, and a low variety in daily talk. No one learns the high variety at home (Ferguson, 1959). His cases were Arabic, Swiss German, Haitian Creole and Modern Greek. Extension to any two languages in a society is CONTESTED.
  - LN.O.A4.2.2 Register. Situation shapes language by field, tenor and mode. Multidimensional analysis of corpus features shows that features co-occur by genre (Biber, 1988). Hypotheses: the corpus represents each genre, and the features counted are comparable across texts.
  - LN.O.A4.2.3 Style and audience. Speakers shift toward the speech of their addressees. Audience design (Bell, 1984) and attention to speech (Labov) are rival accounts of style shift (CONTESTED). A test is the shift when the addressee changes and the topic is held constant.
  - LN.O.A4.2.4 Address terms. In European languages with a familiar and a formal *you*, the choice tracks power and solidarity (Brown and Gilman, 1960). The claimed historical shift from power to solidarity is UNVERIFIED for most languages.
- **LN.O.A4.3 Contact and multilingualism.** **Languages in contact exchange material and structure, and bilingual speakers use their languages in regular ways.** Code-switching; pidgins and creoles.
  - LN.O.A4.3.1 Borrowing. Words are borrowed far more often than grammar. Structural borrowing needs intense contact (Thomason and Kaufman, 1988). Whether any grammatical feature cannot be borrowed is CONTESTED.
  - LN.O.A4.3.2 Code-switching. Switching within a sentence follows grammatical constraints. The equivalence constraint (Poplack, 1980) and the matrix-language-frame model are rival accounts, and each has reported counterexamples (CONTESTED). The test is a corpus of spontaneous bilingual speech.
  - LN.O.A4.3.3 Pidgins and creoles. A pidgin is a reduced contact language with no native speakers. A creole is a contact language that has native speakers and an expanded grammar. Whether creoles form a distinct structural type is CONTESTED. A bioprogram account of creole genesis is also CONTESTED.
  - LN.O.A4.3.4 Bilingualism. The combined vocabulary of bilingual children across both languages is comparable with monolingual vocabulary (UNVERIFIED as to size). Whether bilingualism improves executive control is CONTESTED. The process is MS (uses MS.O.A1.5.3).
- **LN.O.A4.4 Endangerment and revitalisation.** **A language is endangered when it is no longer passed to children, and vitality is measured by stated indicators.** Language shift. Te reo Māori: kōhanga reo from 1982; the Māori Language Act 1987 made it an official language of New Zealand.
  - LN.O.A4.4.1 Language shift. A community shifts when children stop learning the heritage language. Intergenerational transmission is the first factor in UNESCO's 2003 vitality framework. Shift is read from the age profile of speakers.
  - LN.O.A4.4.2 Counting languages. Estimates of the number of living languages, about seven thousand in common catalogues, depend on how languages and dialects are separated. Mutual intelligibility and social criteria give different answers, and no single criterion is decisive (CONTESTED).
  - LN.O.A4.4.3 Documentation. A documentation is a recorded, annotated, translated and archived sample of use, with a grammar and a dictionary drawn from it (Himmelmann, 1998). Hypothesis: the sample covers varied speakers and genres. Conditions of access are set with the speakers' communities. For Indigenous languages the governance is IK (uses IK.O.A5.2).
  - LN.O.A4.4.4 Revitalisation outcomes. A programme is judged by intergenerational transmission after the programme, not by enrolment. Evidence for lasting success is mixed and depends on use in the home. Modern Hebrew is cited as a revived language with native speakers, and its generality as a model is CONTESTED.

Named objects: sociolinguistic variable (variants share meaning, CONTESTED beyond phonology); principle of accountability; apparent-time method (stable adult speech, age-stratified sample); diglossia (stable functional split); register analysis (representative corpus); audience design; borrowing scale; equivalence constraint; matrix-language-frame model; pidgin and creole; language vitality framework; documentary linguistics (varied, archived sample).

### LN.O.A5 Diversity
**SCQA.** **Situation.** The world has several thousand languages. They change, descend from earlier languages and differ in structure within limits. **Complication.** Resemblance between languages comes from descent, contact, chance or a general tendency, and only descent supports a family claim. Claims of deep relationship are disputed, and the origin of language has no direct record. **Question.** Which claims about change, relationship and structural difference between languages does linguistics own? **Answer.** LN owns change within a lineage, genealogical relationship, structural typology and the origin of the language faculty. The last is OPEN.

**Key line** (inductive; plural noun: patterns across languages; dimension: what explains the pattern; order: change within a lineage, then shared ancestry, then structural tendency, then the origin of the faculty). Members (4): Change · Descent and families · Typology and universals · Origin of language. The earlier key line named three members and omitted the origin of language, which was already a sub-cell.

- **LN.O.A5.1 Change.** **Languages change continuously, and sound change is regular in a stated sense.** Regular sound change (the Neogrammarian hypothesis, 1870s); Grimm's law (1822); Verner's law (1875); grammaticalisation.
  - LN.O.A5.1.1 Neogrammarian hypothesis. Sound change is exceptionless given its phonetic conditions, and it applies without regard to individual words. Apparent exceptions come from borrowing, analogy or a further law. Lexical diffusion, in which change spreads word by word, is a rival account (CONTESTED).
  - LN.O.A5.1.2 Grimm's and Verner's laws. Proto-Indo-European voiceless stops became Germanic voiceless fricatives, as in Latin *pater* and English *father*. Verner's law explains the apparent exceptions by the position of the accent in Proto-Indo-European. Hypotheses: the compared words are inherited and the accent is reconstructed correctly.
  - LN.O.A5.1.3 Grammaticalisation. Content words become grammatical markers along recurring paths. English *will* developed from a verb of wanting, and the Romance future from the Latin infinitive plus *habere*. Strict unidirectionality is CONTESTED, since reversals are reported. Paths are judged from attested earlier stages (W7).
  - LN.O.A5.1.4 Rates and sources. Analogy levels irregular forms, and borrowing adds new material. Frequent words are replaced more slowly in Indo-European (Pagel et al., 2007). The rate claim depends on cognate coding and is not shown for all families (UNVERIFIED).
- **LN.O.A5.2 Descent and families.** **Languages are related when they descend from a common ancestor, shown by regular correspondences in inherited material.** The comparative method; Proto-Indo-European; Austronesian, which includes te reo Māori.
  - LN.O.A5.2.1 Comparative method. A parent language is reconstructed from regular sound correspondences. Hypotheses: correspondences are regular, compared words are inherited and enough related word sets exist. Reconstructions are tested by prediction. Hittite later supplied the laryngeal sounds that Saussure had inferred (UNVERIFIED as to detail).
  - LN.O.A5.2.2 Subgrouping. Shared innovations define a subgroup, and shared retentions do not. Hypotheses: the innovations are identified correctly and did not arise independently or spread by contact.
  - LN.O.A5.2.3 Phylogenetic models. Bayesian tree models of cognate sets estimate family trees and dates. Hypotheses: cognates are coded correctly, rates vary in a stated way and borrowing is rare or modelled. The date of Proto-Indo-European is CONTESTED between a steppe and an Anatolian origin. The inference machinery is shared with BI.O.A4.4 (uses BI.O.A4.4).
  - LN.O.A5.2.4 Limits of the method. The comparative method separates inheritance from chance only to a limited time depth, and specialists disagree on the figure. Proposed deep families such as Nostratic are CONTESTED. Mass comparison without regular correspondences is not accepted by most comparativists (CONTESTED). A language with no demonstrated relatives is an isolate, such as Basque.
- **LN.O.A5.3 Typology and universals.** **Languages differ in structure within limits, and cross-linguistic generalisations are tendencies stated with their sample and method.** Greenberg's universals (1963), most of them statistical tendencies rather than exceptionless laws.
  - LN.O.A5.3.1 Word order and implication. Subject–object–verb and subject–verb–object orders together occur in most languages of typological samples. Greenberg's Universal 3 states that verb–subject–object languages have prepositions. Larger later samples show exceptions, so the universal is a tendency.
  - LN.O.A5.3.2 Sampling. A typological claim needs a sample balanced by family and area, because inheritance and contact inflate counts. This is Galton's problem. Catalogues such as the World Atlas of Language Structures are sources, not tests. A claim from an unbalanced sample is weak (W6).
  - LN.O.A5.3.3 Hierarchies. If a language relativises a position on the accessibility hierarchy, it relativises the positions above it (Keenan and Comrie, 1977). The order is subject, direct object, indirect object, oblique, genitive. The hierarchy is an implicational tendency with reported exceptions (UNVERIFIED as to their number).
  - LN.O.A5.3.4 Explaining universals. Innate-faculty accounts and usage-based accounts both claim to explain universal tendencies. Evans and Levinson (2009) argue that few exceptionless universals exist. The choice between accounts is CONTESTED. The acquisition evidence is MS (uses MS.O.A3.3). Whether languages are equally complex overall is CONTESTED.
- **LN.O.A5.4 Origin of language.** **OPEN. How and when the language faculty arose is not settled, and the claims below are constraints or programmes.**
  - LN.O.A5.4.1 Evidence limits. Spoken language leaves no direct record before writing. Any account must fit fossil, archaeological and genetic evidence, all of it indirect (uses BI.O.A4.5).
  - LN.O.A5.4.2 Rival programmes. Gestural-origin, vocal-origin and single-step accounts are OPEN. None is established by direct evidence. Gradual accounts predict intermediate stages in the fossil and archaeological record, and single-step accounts do not.
  - LN.O.A5.4.3 Comparative evidence. Alarm calls, song learning and gesture in other species share features with parts of language. The hypothesis that only recursion is specific to humans (Hauser, Chomsky and Fitch, 2002) is CONTESTED. The animal evidence is BI.O.A3.4 (uses BI.O.A3.4).
  - LN.O.A5.4.4 Emerging languages. New sign languages arise in deaf communities, such as Nicaraguan Sign Language from the late 1970s. They show structure appearing within a few cohorts of learners (UNVERIFIED as to timeline). Their bearing on the origin of language is OPEN.

Named objects: Neogrammarian hypothesis (exceptionless, phonetically conditioned change); Grimm's law; Verner's law (accent position); grammaticalisation path (attested earlier stages); comparative method (regular correspondences, inherited words, enough word sets); subgrouping by shared innovation; Bayesian phylogenetic model (correct cognate coding, stated rate variation, borrowing modelled); Greenberg's universals (balanced sample); accessibility hierarchy; Galton's problem; glottochronology (constant retention rate, CONTESTED, heuristic register).

Worked examples, with hypotheses:
- LN.M.1 Elicit a word list in a fixed frame, record connected texts, and check forms with several speakers. Hypotheses: the consultants speak the variety under study and the elicitation does not prime the form.
- LN.M.2 Count word forms or constructions per million words and test the difference. Hypotheses: the corpus represents the variety, the tokenisation is fixed and the sample is large enough for the rare form.
- LN.M.3 Present minimal pairs to listeners in an identification task, or collect acceptability ratings for balanced sentence sets. Hypotheses: participants do not know the aim, items are counterbalanced and the task does not create the effect.
- LN.M.4 Reconstruct a proto-form from cognate sets, or subgroup languages by shared innovations. Hypotheses are those of LN.O.A5.2.1 and LN.O.A5.2.2.
- LN.M.5 Write a grammar fragment in a stated formalism and test that it generates the acceptable strings and no others. Fit a Bayesian tree to cognate data. Hypotheses are those of LN.O.A5.2.3 and LN.O.A2.4.

### Heuristics register (outside the count)
Glottochronology (CONTESTED, filed here as a heuristic); the native speaker's intuition as a first guess; look for the minimal pair.

Notes. Glottochronology assumes a constant rate of replacement of basic vocabulary, and measured rates vary by word and by language. The linguist's own intuition is a first guess, to be replaced by systematic judgements from several speakers. A minimal pair is a place to start the sound inventory, and the claim still needs the test conditions of LN.O.A1.2.1.
