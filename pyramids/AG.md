# AG — Agriculture, veterinary and food

New field, added at the owner's direction. Standard v2 cited, not copied. Why it is here: ANZSRC 30 had no core home. A crop is not only a species, an animal disorder is not human medicine, and a food standard is not only chemistry. Deepening pass: every A-cell has its own SCQA, a stated key line and named objects with hypotheses. Every sub-cell has a bold claim and second-level cells. Theorems are owned by MA and cited as "uses MA.…".

## 1. SCQA
**Situation.** Agricultural, veterinary and food sciences state how organisms are produced and kept for human use, how their health is maintained, and how food is made safe.
**Complication.** v1 spread these across BI, CH and MD. "Yield of this cultivar under this management" had no home.
**Question.** By what dimension can each claim about producing and keeping organisms and food be placed once?
**Answer.** By the stage of the production system the claim is about: the organism bred, its cultivation or husbandry, its health, the food made from it, and the farming system.

## 2. Governing thought
Agriculture is exhausted by four faces of one pyramid: objects, methods, warrants and bridges.
Its objects divide into five stages of production: breeding, cultivation and husbandry, crop and animal health, food, and farming systems.

## 3. Key line
Logic: inductive. Plural noun: stages of production. Dimension: **the stage of the production system.** Order: from the organism to the whole system.

Members (5):
1. Breeding
2. Cultivation and husbandry
3. Crop and animal health
4. Food
5. Farming systems

Assignment note on the dimension. A thing can change stage, as a pest does when it harms a crop and then a food. The question asked decides the placement. The harm to the crop is A3. The residue in the food is A4. A claim about one farm as a whole is A5 only when it concerns the farm's output, decisions or load, not a single practice.

## 4. Face O — Objects

### AG.O.A1 Breeding
**SCQA.** Situation. Production starts with chosen organisms. The genetic make-up of crops, livestock, trees and farmed fish sets the range that management can reach. Complication. Genetics as a science is BI, and regulation of new techniques is LA. Breeding practice, the use of variation on a stated population for a stated goal, had no home. Question. How are production organisms improved, and how is their variation kept? Answer. AG owns the prediction of response to selection, the techniques that add variation or speed selection, and the conservation of variation.
**Key line** (inductive; plural noun: breeding activities; dimension: what the breeder does with genetic variation; order: use it, add to it, keep it). Members (3): Selection · Genetic improvement · Genetic resources. Key line: selection, genetic improvement, genetic resources.
- **AG.O.A1.1 Selection.** Genetic gain from choosing parents is predicted from heritability, selection intensity and generation interval. The breeder's equation R = h²S predicts response to selection, assuming additive inheritance and a stable environment. The genetics is BI.
  - AG.O.A1.1.1 Realised heritability. The ratio R/S from a selection experiment estimates h² for that population and environment. A different value in a new environment shows genotype-by-environment interaction or non-additive effects (uses BI.O.A4.2).
  - AG.O.A1.1.2 Annual genetic gain. Gain per year is accuracy times intensity times additive genetic standard deviation, divided by generation interval. Hypotheses: normally distributed breeding values, stable variance, selection on one criterion.
  - AG.O.A1.1.3 Selection index. The Smith–Hazel index weights several traits to maximise correlation with total merit. Hypotheses: known genetic and phenotypic covariances, set economic weights, additive effects. Wrong weights lower the gain in total merit.
  - AG.O.A1.1.4 Breeding value prediction. Best linear unbiased prediction estimates breeding values from records and relationships. Hypotheses: known relationships, known or well-estimated variance components, a linear mixed model that fits. Accuracy is tested against later records.
- **AG.O.A1.2 Genetic improvement.** A technique that adds variation or speeds selection is judged by the performance of its products in the target environment. Hybrids, marker-assisted selection, gene editing. Regulation is LA.
  - AG.O.A1.2.1 Heterosis. F1 hybrids can outperform both parent lines, and the size of the gain depends on the parents and the trait. The advantage falls in later generations as heterozygosity falls. The genetic explanation (dominance, overdominance, epistasis) is CONTESTED.
  - AG.O.A1.2.2 Marker-assisted and genomic selection. Marker-assisted selection tracks a major-effect locus and works while marker and locus stay associated in the breeding population (uses BI.O.A1.3). Genomic selection fits many markers jointly. Its accuracy falls as the training population diverges from the selection candidates.
  - AG.O.A1.2.3 Gene editing. A targeted edit changes the trait only if it has the expected effect in the genetic background. Off-target changes are checked by sequencing. The mutation is BI; the legal status of edited organisms is LA (uses BI.O.A1.4, LA.O.A1.1).
  - AG.O.A1.2.4 Transgenic events. A genetically modified line is evaluated event by event, because insertion site and expression affect performance. Equivalence to the conventional line is shown by trial on stated traits and is not assumed.
- **AG.O.A1.3 Genetic resources.** A collection keeps the variation of its source population only as far as sample size and effective population size allow. Seed banks, landraces.
  - AG.O.A1.3.1 Seed storage. Orthodox seeds survive drying and cold storage. Recalcitrant seeds do not and need other storage. Within a tested range, longevity falls as moisture and temperature rise, as in the Ellis–Roberts viability equations. Hypotheses: orthodox behaviour, constants fitted for the species.
  - AG.O.A1.3.2 Effective population size and inbreeding. Inbreeding per generation is about 1/(2Ne) in an idealised population. Hypotheses: random mating, constant size, no selection. Real Ne is usually below the census count (uses BI.O.A4.1). Wright's coefficient from a pedigree needs a correct, deep pedigree and unrelated founders.
  - AG.O.A1.3.3 Landraces. A landrace may hold alleles absent from modern cultivars. This is tested by genotyping and by trial, with the diversity measure named, such as allelic richness or expected heterozygosity.
  - AG.O.A1.3.4 Core collections. A core collection is a subset that represents a larger collection at a stated fraction. Representativeness is tested against the full collection with a named diversity measure on markers or traits.

Named objects: breeder's equation (hypotheses: additive effects, stable environment, h² from the same population); annual-gain form of the breeder's equation; Smith–Hazel selection index (known covariances, set weights); best linear unbiased prediction (known relationships, linear mixed model); heterosis (CONTESTED mechanism); genomic selection (training population related to candidates); effective population size (idealised population); Ellis–Roberts seed viability equations (orthodox seed); core collection.

### AG.O.A2 Cultivation and husbandry
**SCQA.** Situation. Organisms are grown and kept under managed conditions: on soil, with water, by crop practice, in animal systems, and in farmed waters and forests. Complication. Results differ with site, season and population, so a result from one trial is not a general law. NZ pastoral dairying is a substrate, not a rule for all husbandry. Question. What management gives what result, under which stated conditions? Answer. AG owns the response of production to management, with site, season and population stated. The soil's state is EA, and the organism's biology is BI.
**Key line** (inductive; plural noun: management domains; dimension: the resource or organism being managed; order: inputs to the crop, then the animal, then farmed waters and forests). Members (5): Soils and nutrients · Water and irrigation · Crop management · Animal husbandry · Aquaculture, fisheries and forestry production. Key line: soils and nutrients, water, crop management, animal husbandry, aquaculture and forestry.
- **AG.O.A2.1 Soils and nutrients.** Yield rises with nutrient supply at a falling rate until another factor limits. Fertiliser response. Liebig's law of the minimum holds as a first approximation, when one nutrient limits growth. The soil's state is EA.
  - AG.O.A2.1.1 Law of the minimum. Growth is set by the scarcest essential resource. Hypotheses: one factor limits at a time, factors do not substitute, factors do not interact. Interaction between nutrients is common, so the law is an approximation.
  - AG.O.A2.1.2 Response curves. Linear-plateau, quadratic-plateau and Mitscherlich forms fit the same trial data differently. The estimated optimum rate depends on the form chosen. Hypothesis for the Mitscherlich form: gain is proportional to the shortfall from the maximum, with other factors not limiting.
  - AG.O.A2.1.3 Soil test calibration. A soil test value guides fertiliser only if calibrated against crop response for that soil group, crop and extraction method, such as Olsen phosphorus. Tissue ratio norms, as in DRIS, need a high-yielding reference population of the same crop and growth stage (uses EA.O.A4.5, CH.O.A5.3).
  - AG.O.A2.1.4 Nutrient recovery. Apparent recovery is the extra uptake from fertiliser divided by the dose. It varies with soil, season and timing, and is not a constant. Legumes supply nitrogen through fixation, which falls when soil mineral nitrogen is high.
- **AG.O.A2.2 Water and irrigation.** Crop water use is estimated from weather and crop stage. Yield loss from a shortfall depends on crop and stage.
  - AG.O.A2.2.1 Crop water requirement. Crop evapotranspiration is a weather-based reference value times a crop coefficient (FAO-56). Hypotheses: the weather data represent the field, and the coefficient fits the crop, stage and climate (uses EA.O.A4.2).
  - AG.O.A2.2.2 Yield response to water. Relative yield loss is taken as proportional to relative evapotranspiration deficit through a response factor. Hypotheses: the factor fits the crop and growth stage, and water is the only limit.
  - AG.O.A2.2.3 Irrigation scheduling. Water is applied when root-zone depletion reaches a chosen share of available water. Hypotheses: known root depth and soil water limits. Deficit irrigation raises water productivity only if stress falls in stages tolerant of it, which is tested by trial.
  - AG.O.A2.2.4 Salinity and sodicity. Sodium adsorption ratio compares sodium with calcium and magnesium in milliequivalents per litre. High values harm soil structure. Yield falls linearly above a crop-specific salinity threshold in the Maas–Hoffman form (uses EA.O.A4.5).
- **AG.O.A2.3 Crop management.** A practice changes yield, and the claim holds only for the stated season, site and population. Rotation, tillage, sowing. A yield claim must state season, site and population.
  - AG.O.A2.3.1 Rotation. The rotation effect is yield after a given preceding crop compared with continuous cropping in the same long-term trial. Its sign depends on crop sequence, disease carry-over and nitrogen left by legumes.
  - AG.O.A2.3.2 Tillage. Pooled comparisons report a lower average yield under no-till than under conventional tillage. The gap narrows where residues are kept and crops are rotated. The size of the average gap is UNVERIFIED and depends on soil and climate.
  - AG.O.A2.3.3 Intercropping. The land equivalent ratio sums each crop's mixture yield divided by its sole-crop yield. A value above one means the mixture uses land better. Hypotheses: sole crops at their own best density and management, same site and season.
  - AG.O.A2.3.4 Phenology and sowing date. Crop development is modelled by thermal time accumulated above a base temperature. Hypotheses: development is linear between base and upper thresholds, and the thresholds are known for the cultivar (uses EA.O.A4.3).
- **AG.O.A2.4 Animal husbandry.** Animal performance is set by feed intake and its use, within a husbandry system and a welfare standard. Pastoral and housed systems, nutrition, welfare. NZ pastoral dairying is a substrate.
  - AG.O.A2.4.1 Energy requirement. Requirement for metabolisable energy is summed over maintenance, growth, pregnancy and lactation. Hypotheses: feed energy is measured on representative samples, and the requirement equations fit the animal class. Feed value by laboratory proxy needs calibration against animal trials.
  - AG.O.A2.4.2 Stocking rate. Over a moderate range and at a given pasture supply, output per animal falls and output per hectare rises as stocking rate rises. The range and the turning point are tested by farmlet trials.
  - AG.O.A2.4.3 Rumen digestion and methane. Rumen microbes ferment fibre and release methane. Enteric methane per animal rises with feed intake. Emission per unit of product falls as productivity per animal rises, which is UNVERIFIED across systems. The impact is EV.
  - AG.O.A2.4.4 Welfare measurement. Welfare is assessed by animal-based measures such as body condition score, lameness score and behaviour. Hypotheses: the scale suits the species and breed, and assessors are trained. A framework such as the Five Freedoms is a convention (W12), and welfare law is LA (uses LA.O.A1.1).
- **AG.O.A2.5 Aquaculture, fisheries and forestry production.** Harvest is sustainable only if removal does not exceed production. Stock and stand models state the assumptions that make this checkable.
  - AG.O.A2.5.1 Surplus production. Maximum sustainable yield follows from logistic growth. Hypotheses: logistic growth, equilibrium, catch per unit effort proportional to abundance (uses BI.O.A5.1). Maximum sustainable yield as a management target is CONTESTED.
  - AG.O.A2.5.2 Catch per unit effort. Catch per unit effort is an abundance index only if catchability is constant and effort is standardised. When fish aggregate, the index stays high as the stock falls, which is called hyperstability.
  - AG.O.A2.5.3 Forest stand growth. Stand volume follows a sigmoid curve. The age that maximises mean annual increment is where mean annual increment equals current annual increment. Hypotheses: a single stand, a growth curve for the site, and volume as the only objective.
  - AG.O.A2.5.4 Feed conversion in aquaculture. Feed conversion ratio is feed given divided by live weight gained. It varies with species, temperature, size and feeding rate. Hypothesis: weights are taken over the same period and mortality is accounted for.

Named objects: Liebig's law of the minimum (one limiting factor, no interaction); Mitscherlich response (other factors not limiting); soil test calibration; DRIS (reference population of the same crop); FAO-56 crop coefficient method (weather represents field); yield response factor (water the only limit); sodium adsorption ratio; Maas–Hoffman salinity response; land equivalent ratio; thermal time model; stocking rate; body condition scoring; Beverton–Holt yield-per-recruit (constant recruitment, known growth and natural mortality, steady state); virtual population analysis (known natural mortality, accurate catch at age, closed stock); surplus production model; mean annual increment; feed conversion ratio.

### AG.O.A3 Crop and animal health
**SCQA.** Situation. Producing organisms fall ill and are attacked by pests, weeds and pathogens. Loss is limited by detection, diagnosis and control. Complication. Veterinary medicine borders human medicine, and BI.O.A3.5 also lists animal disorder. Control agents select for resistance, and border measures partly belong to law. Question. How is the health of crops and animals kept, and what evidence shows that control works? Answer. AG owns the detection, diagnosis and control of harm to production organisms, with the spread models behind them. The pathogen's biology is BI, and human disease is MD.
**Key line** (inductive; plural noun: health measures; dimension: what is protected and where the measure acts; order: the crop, the animal, the border, then the animal–human interface). Members (4): Plant protection · Veterinary medicine · Biosecurity · Zoonoses. Key line: plant protection, veterinary medicine, biosecurity, zoonoses.
- **AG.O.A3.1 Plant protection.** Control is justified when pest density passes the level at which control pays. Resistance to a control is a selection response. Pests, weeds, pathogens; integrated pest management.
  - AG.O.A3.1.1 Economic injury level. The injury level is control cost divided by crop value times damage per pest times control efficacy. The economic threshold is set below it to leave time to act. Hypotheses: damage is linear in density, and cost, value and efficacy are known and constant.
  - AG.O.A3.1.2 Resistance management. Resistance to a pesticide or a resistant cultivar evolves under selection (uses BI.O.A4.1). The high-dose and refuge strategy delays it. Hypotheses: resistance is recessive and rare, and survivors mate at random with refuge insects.
  - AG.O.A3.1.3 Biological control. Classical biological control introduces a natural enemy from the pest's region of origin. Success is shown by lower pest density against a comparison site. Host specificity is tested before release (uses BI.O.A5.2).
  - AG.O.A3.1.4 Critical period of weed control. The critical period is the stage window in which weeds must be removed to keep yield loss within a set limit. Its length depends on crop, weed density and relative time of emergence.
- **AG.O.A3.2 Veterinary medicine.** A veterinary claim states the species, the test or intervention, and its measured accuracy or effect. Diagnosis and treatment of animal disorders. CONTESTED: BI.O.A3.5 also claims animal disorder, diagnosis and treatment; the alternative for all disorders is MD.
  - AG.O.A3.2.1 Test accuracy in animals. Sensitivity and specificity are measured against a reference standard, and predictive values change with prevalence (uses MD.O.A3.2). The Rogan–Gladen estimator corrects apparent prevalence. Hypothesis: sensitivity and specificity are known and constant across populations.
  - AG.O.A3.2.2 Withholding periods. A withholding period is set so that residues in milk or meat fall below a limit after the last dose. It comes from depletion studies on the species and product. The safety limit is MD (uses MD.O.A4.4.1).
  - AG.O.A3.2.3 Antimicrobial use and resistance. Antimicrobial use in an animal group raises the frequency of resistance in its bacteria (uses MD.O.A2.1.1). The share of human resistance traced to animal use differs by organism and is CONTESTED.
  - AG.O.A3.2.4 Trials in veterinary practice. The herd, not the animal, is the unit of randomisation when infection spreads between animals. Hypotheses of the randomised trial: random assignment, no spillover between units, adequate sample size.
- **AG.O.A3.3 Biosecurity.** Biosecurity lowers the chance and cost of an organism entering, establishing or spreading. Its measures are judged by detection and eradication outcomes. Border and incursion management (e.g. Mycoplasma bovis in NZ from 2017, UNVERIFIED date).
  - AG.O.A3.3.1 Inspection and sampling. A clean sample of n units from a large lot gives only an upper bound of about 3/n on the infested fraction, at 95 per cent confidence (uses MA.O.A5). Hypotheses: random, independent sampling and a perfect test.
  - AG.O.A3.3.2 Surveillance sensitivity. For n units sampled at random, the chance of detecting disease at design prevalence p is 1 − (1 − Se·p)^n, with test sensitivity Se (uses MA.O.A5). A claim of freedom depends on the assumed design prevalence.
  - AG.O.A3.3.3 Eradication feasibility. Eradication needs the effective reproduction number held below one (uses MD.O.A5.2). Spread between farms follows movement networks, and heterogeneous contact can invalidate a homogeneous-mixing threshold (uses MD.O.A5.2.3).
  - AG.O.A3.3.4 Establishment of introduced pests. The chance of establishment rises with propagule pressure, the number of individuals and of release events. The harm to native ecosystems is EV (uses EV.O.A1.4). The harm to production is here.
- **AG.O.A3.4 Zoonoses.** A zoonosis is an infection shared by animals and people, and AG owns the animal-host side of its maintenance and control. The animal side is AG; human disease is MD (One Health is a bridge).
  - AG.O.A3.4.1 Reservoirs. A reservoir is a population that maintains the pathogen without outside input. It is identified by infection data from the host and evidence that the host supplies transmission. Brushtail possums and bovine tuberculosis in NZ are a standard case.
  - AG.O.A3.4.2 Control at source. Vaccinating or culling animals lowers human cases only if the animal route dominates. This is tested by human incidence before and after control, against a comparison (uses MD.O.A5.1).
  - AG.O.A3.4.3 Spillover. Predicting which animal pathogens will spill over to people is an OPEN programme. Contact rate, prevalence and infection per contact are measured, but host traits predict emergence poorly (UNVERIFIED strength).
  - AG.O.A3.4.4 Foodborne zoonoses. On-farm control and processing control contribute separately to human foodborne cases. Source attribution apportions cases using stated model assumptions (uses MD.O.A5.1).

Named objects: economic injury level (damage linear in density, constants known); economic threshold; high-dose and refuge strategy (recessive rare resistance, random mating); classical biological control; critical period of weed control; Rogan–Gladen estimator (known sensitivity and specificity); withholding period; rule of three for zero-positive samples (random sampling); surveillance sensitivity formula; design prevalence; reservoir; propagule pressure; One Health.

### AG.O.A4 Food
**SCQA.** Situation. Produce becomes food through composition, processing, safety control and specification. Complication. The molecule is CH, human nutritional need is MD, and the legal force of a standard is LA. v1 blended these in one cell. Question. What makes food safe, nutritious and stable, and how is that stated so it can be checked? Answer. AG owns the composition of food as a product, the kinetics of processing, the control of hazards, and the standards as stipulations. Chemistry, human need and law keep their own claims.
**Key line** (inductive; plural noun: food properties and controls; dimension: what is asserted about the food; order: what it is, how it is kept stable, how it is made safe, how it is specified). Members (4): Composition and nutrition · Processing and preservation · Food safety · Food standards. Key line: composition and nutrition, processing, safety, standards.
- **AG.O.A4.1 Composition and nutrition.** The composition of a food is a measured amount of components by a stated method. It varies with variety, season and processing. The molecule is CH; human nutritional need is MD.
  - AG.O.A4.1.1 Proximate analysis. Moisture, ash, protein, fat and carbohydrate by difference are reported by stated methods. Protein is Kjeldahl nitrogen times a conversion factor, commonly 6.25, which assumes a typical nitrogen share (uses CH.O.A5.3). Oven drying needs constant mass and negligible loss of other volatiles.
  - AG.O.A4.1.2 Natural variation. A composition table gives a mean with variation. One sample does not equal the table value. A label claim must state its basis, such as per 100 g as sold or as eaten.
  - AG.O.A4.1.3 Retention in processing. Nutrient loss on cooking and storage is described by measured retention factors for a stated food, temperature and time. Hypothesis for a first-order model: loss rate is proportional to the amount present (uses CH.O.A4.1).
  - AG.O.A4.1.4 Food security measures. The Food Insecurity Experience Scale uses Rasch scaling, with one underlying dimension, equal item discrimination and independent responses. CONTESTED: alternative homes are EC and MD.O.A5.
- **AG.O.A4.2 Processing and preservation.** Preservation keeps food safe and stable by lowering water activity, temperature or pH, or by killing or inhibiting microbes. Each effect is quantified. Fermentation, pasteurisation, drying.
  - AG.O.A4.2.1 Thermal death time. The D value is the time at a temperature for one tenfold reduction. The z value is the temperature change that changes D tenfold. Hypotheses: first-order death, known values for the target in that food, heat reaching the coldest point (uses CH.O.A4.2).
  - AG.O.A4.2.2 Water activity. Growth is not possible below a limiting water activity that differs by organism. Drying and salting act by lowering it. The moisture sorption isotherm links water content to water activity and shows hysteresis.
  - AG.O.A4.2.3 Pasteurisation. Pasteurisation is a stated time and temperature that gives a set reduction of target pathogens without sterilising. Milk pasteurisation is verified with the alkaline phosphatase test. Hypothesis: target organism and D and z values are chosen correctly.
  - AG.O.A4.2.4 Fermentation yield and growth. Ethanol yield from glucose cannot exceed 0.511 g per g by stoichiometry (uses CH.O.A1.2). Real yields are lower. Monod kinetics assume one limiting substrate and balanced growth (uses BI.O.A2.3).
- **AG.O.A4.3 Food safety.** A food is safe in a stated sense when hazards are controlled to the level a standard allows. This is shown by verified controls, not by end-product testing alone. HACCP as a control system (W11); pathogen limits.
  - AG.O.A4.3.1 HACCP principles. HACCP has seven principles: hazard analysis, critical control points, critical limits, monitoring, corrective action, verification and record keeping. Hypotheses: hazards are identified correctly, prerequisite programmes operate, and critical limits are validated.
  - AG.O.A4.3.2 End-product testing. The chance of detecting a contaminated lot is one minus (1 − p)^n for n random samples at contamination proportion p (uses MA.O.A5). Hypothesis: random spread of the contaminant. Clustering makes a sampling plan overstate assurance.
  - AG.O.A4.3.3 Predictive microbiology. Primary models give growth over time. Secondary models give growth rate against temperature, pH and water activity. Hypotheses: conditions lie within the fitted range, and the strain behaves like the fitted strains. Challenge tests check the model.
  - AG.O.A4.3.4 Chemical hazards. Mycotoxins in grain are concentrated in few kernels, so sampling error dominates the measured concentration. Residues, allergens and mycotoxins are controlled by limits and by sampling plans with stated uncertainty.
- **AG.O.A4.4 Food standards.** A food standard is a stipulated specification (W12). Its content is set by a standard-setting body, and its legal force is a legal claim. Codex Alimentarius; FSANZ for Australia and NZ (W12; legal force LA).
  - AG.O.A4.4.1 Codex Alimentarius. Codex texts are international standards, guidelines and codes of practice. They bind a state only when adopted. The WTO Agreement on the Application of Sanitary and Phytosanitary Measures refers to them (uses LA.O.A1.4).
  - AG.O.A4.4.2 Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code. FSANZ develops the Code. The Code has legal effect through legislation in each jurisdiction (uses LA.O.A1.1). The content of a limit is AG, and its enforcement is LA.
  - AG.O.A4.4.3 Maximum residue limits. A limit rests on supervised residue trials under good practice, then a check that estimated intake stays below a health-based reference value (uses MD). Compliance is tested by sampling and analysis with stated measurement uncertainty.
  - AG.O.A4.4.4 Labelling and certification. Organic certification verifies a production process against a written standard and does not guarantee a residue-free product. Origin and nutrient claims are definitions (W12) checked by inspection.

Named objects: proximate analysis; Kjeldahl method (nitrogen-to-protein factor assumed); food composition table; thermal death time D and z (first-order death, coldest point reached); water activity; pasteurisation; Monod growth model (one limiting substrate, balanced growth); HACCP (hazards correctly identified, validated limits); predictive microbiology (fitted range); Codex standard; FSANZ Code; maximum residue limit; organic certification.

### AG.O.A5 Farming systems
**SCQA.** Situation. Farms are systems of land, organisms, labour and markets, and outcomes arise from the interaction of the parts. Complication. Farm economics and prices are EC, and environmental impact is EV. Productivity measurement and farm decisions sit between them and had no single home. Question. How are a farm's output, decisions and environmental load measured and placed? Answer. AG owns the measurement of farm physical productivity, the decision methods applied to farms, and the practice-level load that farming creates. EC owns prices and welfare, and EV owns the impact.
**Key line** (inductive; plural noun: whole-farm questions; dimension: what is asked of the farm as a whole; order: what it produces, how it is run, what it releases). Members (3): System productivity · Farm management · Environmental effect of farming. Key line: system productivity, farm management, environmental effect.
- **AG.O.A5.1 System productivity.** Farm productivity is a ratio of aggregate output to aggregate input. Measured change depends on the index and the inputs counted. Total factor productivity of farms (measurement here; economics EC).
  - AG.O.A5.1.1 Partial and total productivity. Yield per hectare and output per worker rise when other inputs substitute, so partial measures do not show efficiency. A total factor index weights inputs by cost share. Cost shares stand for output elasticities only under competitive input markets and constant returns (uses EC.O.A4.1).
  - AG.O.A5.1.2 Yield gap. The yield gap is potential yield from a validated crop model minus actual yield. Hypotheses: the model is validated for the site and cultivar, and the weather record represents the period. Part of the gap is not economically closable.
  - AG.O.A5.1.3 Livestock system efficiency. Output per unit of feed or per hectare ranks systems differently when the system boundary changes, such as with or without imported feed. A comparison must state the boundary and the feed accounted for.
- **AG.O.A5.2 Farm management.** A farm decision is evaluated by what changes in returns and costs, with risk stated. Decisions under weather and price risk.
  - AG.O.A5.2.1 Gross margin and partial budget. Gross margin is enterprise output minus variable costs. A partial budget counts only the costs and returns that change. Hypotheses: variable costs are allocable, fixed costs are unchanged, everything else stays the same (uses EC).
  - AG.O.A5.2.2 Investment appraisal. Net present value discounts forecast cash flows. Hypotheses: the cash flows are forecast with stated error, and the discount rate reflects the risk. A positive value is a forecast, not a result.
  - AG.O.A5.2.3 Income risk. Diversification lowers income variance only if enterprise returns are not perfectly positively correlated (uses MA.O.A5). Insurance and forward contracts transfer risk and carry a cost. The claim is tested on farm records.
  - AG.O.A5.2.4 Whole-farm models. A whole-farm linear programme assumes linear constraints, known coefficients and one objective (uses MA). Its optimum is optimal for the model. It is a decision aid and is not evidence about the farm.
- **AG.O.A5.3 Environmental effect of farming.** AG owns the practice and the farm-level load it produces, and EV owns the effect on the receiving environment. Nitrate leaching, methane. The impact claim is EV; AG owns the practice that changes it.
  - AG.O.A5.3.1 Nitrogen balance. Farm-gate nitrogen surplus is inputs minus nitrogen in products. It indicates loss potential and does not give the loss path. Hypotheses: the boundary is defined and all significant flows are counted. Urine patches in grazed pasture concentrate the load (uses EV.O.A2.2).
  - AG.O.A5.3.2 Methane intensity and total. Emission per unit of product can fall while total emission rises if output rises. Both figures must be stated. Inventories use emission factors per animal class (uses EV.O.A1.1).
  - AG.O.A5.3.3 Mitigation effect. A mitigation practice, such as a nitrification inhibitor or riparian planting, has an effect size that depends on site. A plot-scale result need not hold at farm or catchment scale. Transfer is tested by trial at the larger scale.
  - AG.O.A5.3.4 Soil carbon change. Practice changes soil organic carbon slowly compared with sampling error. Detection needs paired sampling on an equivalent soil mass basis, many samples and several years (uses EA.O.A4.5).

Named objects: total factor productivity (competitive input markets, constant returns for the cost-share reading); yield gap (validated crop model); gross margin; partial budgeting (other things unchanged); net present value (forecast flows, risk-based discount rate); whole-farm linear programme (linear, known coefficients); nitrogen balance (defined boundary); mass balance; equivalent soil mass sampling.

## 5. Face M — Methods

| ID | Role | Shared role | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| AG.M.1 | Field trial | M4 Intervene | randomised plot and herd trials |
| AG.M.2 | Survey and monitor | M3 Observe | farm surveys, disease surveillance |
| AG.M.3 | Breed and select | M4 Intervene | breeding programmes |
| AG.M.4 | Model systems | M5 Compute | crop and farm-system models |
| AG.M.5 | Inspect and certify | M7 Verify | food inspection, organic certification |

Hypotheses and field examples for each role.
- AG.M.1 Field trial. Hypotheses: random assignment of treatments to plots or herds, no spillover between units, plots representative of the target, enough replication. Examples: a randomised complete block design for cultivars (AG.O.A1.2); a nitrogen rate trial across sites (AG.O.A2.1); a herd-level vaccine trial (AG.O.A3.2.4).
- AG.M.2 Survey and monitor. Hypotheses: a defined sampling frame, probability sampling, known test sensitivity. Examples: bulk milk testing and sentinel herds (AG.O.A3.3.2); a farm-gate nitrogen survey (AG.O.A5.3.1); pest trapping networks.
- AG.M.3 Breed and select. Hypotheses: an additive genetic model, known relationships, phenotypes recorded on relatives of candidates. Examples: progeny testing and genomic evaluation (AG.O.A1.1.4, AG.O.A1.2.2).
- AG.M.4 Model systems. Hypotheses: parameters calibrated for cultivar and soil, validation on independent data, weather inputs that represent the site. Examples: crop growth models such as APSIM (AG.O.A5.1.2); whole-farm programmes (AG.O.A5.2.4); stock assessment models (AG.O.A2.5).
- AG.M.5 Inspect and certify. Hypotheses: a written standard, a stated sampling plan, an independent assessor. Examples: HACCP verification (AG.O.A4.3.1); organic certification (AG.O.A4.4.4); official meat and dairy inspection.

### Heuristics register (outside the count)
"Feed the soil, not the plant"; right product, right rate, right time, right place; farmer knowledge as a first guess. Each is a prompt for a trial, not a result. "Right rate" depends on the response curve at the site (AG.O.A2.1.2), so the rule gives no number.

## 6. Face W — Warrants

| Cell | Warrant | Standard | Typical defeaters |
|---|---|---|---|
| AG.O.A1 | W3 + W4 | replicated trials across environments | genotype-by-environment interaction |
| AG.O.A2 | W3 | replicated field trial with stated site and season | site specificity, weather years |
| AG.O.A3 | W3 + W4 | controlled trial; surveillance with stated sensitivity | under-reporting |
| AG.O.A4 | W4 + W11 + W12 | laboratory analysis; conformity with standard | sampling error |
| AG.O.A5 | W4 + W2 | farm data; system model with validation | model scope |

Field examples for each row.
- AG.O.A1. A cultivar ranking is accepted from trials in several sites and seasons. The ranking fails if it changes between sites. Genomic prediction owes W2 with a stated accuracy from cross-validation.
- AG.O.A2. A fertiliser rate claim owes a replicated rate trial at the stated soil and season. A claim from one wet year is defeated by a dry year. A stock model claim owes W2 and its assumptions.
- AG.O.A3. A control claim owes a trial against an untreated comparison. A freedom-from-disease claim owes surveillance with a stated design prevalence and sensitivity (W4). Under-reporting by farmers lowers the apparent incidence.
- AG.O.A4. A composition claim owes a laboratory result with its method and uncertainty (W4). A safety claim owes verified controls (W11). A limit is a stipulation (W12). A single negative lot test is defeated by clustered contamination.
- AG.O.A5. A productivity claim owes farm records with a stated index and boundary (W4). A yield gap claim owes a validated model (W2). A model fitted to one region is defeated outside its scope.
