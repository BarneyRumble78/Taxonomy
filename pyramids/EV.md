# EV — Environmental science

New field, added at the owner's direction. Standard v2 cited, not copied. Why it is here: ANZSRC 41 is not the same as Earth science. A process claim (EA) and a harm claim were forced into one cell.

## 1. SCQA
**Situation.** Environmental science studies coupled human and natural systems: what people do to the environment, what state results, what harm follows, and what is done about it.
**Complication.** EA owns how the Earth works and BI owns how ecosystems work. Nothing owned "this pressure causes this harm to this system", which is the core environmental claim.
**Question.** By what dimension can each coupled claim be placed once, with the process science left to EA, BI and CH?
**Answer.** By the link in the causal chain the claim concerns: pressure, state, impact, response or limit. This is an adaptation of the EEA's DPSIR framework, with drivers folded into pressures and limits added.

## 2. Governing thought
Environmental science is exhausted by four faces of one pyramid: objects, methods, warrants and bridges.
Its objects divide into five links of a coupled system: pressures, states, impacts, responses, and limits.

## 3. Key line
Dimension: **the link in the human–environment causal chain.** Order: causal.
1. Pressures
2. States
3. Impacts
4. Responses
5. Limits

## 4. Face O — Objects

Each A-cell gives a Situation, Complication, Question and Answer, then its key line, its sub-cells and the named objects it owns. Each sub-cell opens with a bold claim. Child cells below a sub-cell state one testable claim each. Where a model or method is named, its hypotheses are stated in the same sentence (R5).

### EV.O.A1 Pressures

**SCQA.** Situation. People load the environment by what they release, convert, remove and introduce. Complication. Loads are described by activity (farming, transport), by medium (air, water) and by receptor, and v1 mixed the three. A load was also confused with its effect. Question. Which loads, from which activities, in what units? Answer. A pressure claim names a flux or a change imposed by people, in stated units over a stated period. It does not assert the effect, which is A3. Drivers, the activities that create a load, are grouped under pressures and are not a sixth key-line cell.

Key line (inductive; plural noun: pressure types; dimension: what people do to the environment; order: structural, from material added to organisms added). Members (4): Emissions · Land and sea use change · Extraction · Introduced species.

- EV.O.A1.1 Emissions — greenhouse gases, pollutants. **An emission is a flux of a substance or energy from a source to the environment, stated per period and per unit of activity.** Inventories follow IPCC guidelines (W12 for method).
  - EV.O.A1.1.1 Greenhouse gas inventories. IPCC guidelines define tiers. Tier 1 multiplies activity data by a default emission factor. Higher tiers use country-specific factors or process models. Hypotheses: the activity data are complete, and the factor represents the local process.
  - EV.O.A1.1.2 Warming potential metrics. Global warming potential converts a gas to carbon dioxide equivalent over a stated horizon, commonly 100 years. The horizon changes the weight of short-lived gases such as methane. Hypotheses: a pulse emission, a fixed horizon and carbon dioxide as reference. The convention is W12; the radiative physics is EA.O.A4.3.
  - EV.O.A1.1.3 Primary and secondary pollutants. Sulphur dioxide and particles leave a source directly. Ground-level ozone forms in the air from precursors, so its concentration is a state (EV.O.A2.1), not an emission. The formation chemistry is CH.O.A4.5.
  - EV.O.A1.1.4 Point and diffuse discharges. A point source is measured at the outfall. A diffuse source is estimated from land use, rainfall and soil. A load is flow times concentration integrated over the period, so a concentration alone is not a load.
- EV.O.A1.2 Land and sea use change — clearance, drainage, urbanisation. **A land or sea use change is a documented conversion of cover or management, measured as area by class between two dates.**
  - EV.O.A1.2.1 Cover conversion from imagery. Change is reported from classified imagery, with accuracy stated by a confusion matrix. Area of change counted from mapped pixels is biased by classification error. An unbiased area needs a sample-based reference estimate.
  - EV.O.A1.2.2 Habitat loss and fragmentation. Loss of habitat area and the splitting of the remainder are separate pressures. Whether fragmentation affects richness beyond the loss of area is CONTESTED (uses BI.O.A5.5).
  - EV.O.A1.2.3 Hydrological modification. Drainage, damming and channelisation alter the flow regime: its magnitude, timing and frequency. Replacing permeable with impervious cover raises peak runoff for a given rainfall. Flow as a water-balance term is EA.O.A4.2.
  - EV.O.A1.2.4 Seabed and coastal disturbance. Bottom trawling, dredging, reclamation and farm structures alter the seabed. The pressure is the area and frequency of disturbance. The effect on benthic communities is a later claim (EV.O.A3.1).
- EV.O.A1.3 Extraction — water, fish, minerals. **An extraction is a removal of water, organisms or minerals from a stock, stated as a rate against the stock's size or renewal.** Fishing and the fishery are one taking, not two cells.
  - EV.O.A1.3.1 Water abstraction. A take is consumptive when the water is not returned to its source. Allocation is measured as volume per period against flow or recharge. A legal allocation is LA; the hydrology is EA.O.A4.2.
  - EV.O.A1.3.2 Harvest of living stocks. Harvest pressure is catch relative to stock size or production. The Schaefer surplus production model gives maximum sustainable yield. Hypotheses: logistic growth, equilibrium, and catch per unit effort proportional to abundance. Maximum sustainable yield as a target is CONTESTED (uses BI.O.A5.1). The production system is AG.O.A2.5.
  - EV.O.A1.3.3 Mineral and aggregate extraction. Mining and quarrying remove a non-renewable stock and create waste rock and tailings. The pressure is the area disturbed and the mass moved. Deposit geology is EA.O.A1.5.
  - EV.O.A1.3.4 Forest and biomass removal. Removal is volume per area per year against the growth increment. The Faustmann rotation gives the harvest age that maximises land value. Hypotheses: a known growth function, constant prices, costs and interest rate, and replanting after each harvest (uses EC).
- EV.O.A1.4 Introduced species — pests and invasives (e.g. possums and mustelids in NZ). **An introduction is a documented arrival of a species outside its historical range through human agency, with the pathway recorded.**
  - EV.O.A1.4.1 Pathways and vectors. Deliberate pathways include horticulture and acclimatisation. Accidental ones include hull fouling, ballast water and contaminated goods. Pathway risk is counted as arrivals per pathway per year.
  - EV.O.A1.4.2 Propagule pressure. Propagule pressure is the number of individuals and of release events. Higher propagule pressure raises the probability of establishment. Border measures that lower it are AG.O.A3.3.
  - EV.O.A1.4.3 Invasion stages. An invasion passes through transport, introduction, establishment and spread. The fraction passing each stage is estimated from records. The tens rule, about one in ten per stage, is a heuristic and its fit varies by taxon (UNVERIFIED).
  - EV.O.A1.4.4 Pest density as pressure. A pest's damage depends on its density. Control aims at a density below a stated damage threshold. For possums and mustelids the pressure is predator density, and the effect on prey is EV.O.A3.1 (uses BI.O.A5.2).

Named objects: emission factor and activity data (hypotheses above), global warming potential (pulse emission, fixed horizon, carbon dioxide as reference), load (flow times concentration), land-cover class and confusion matrix, flow regime, propagule pressure, surplus production model (logistic growth, equilibrium, catch per unit effort proportional to abundance), material flow analysis (system boundary and period defined, every significant flow counted), Faustmann rotation (uses EC).

### EV.O.A2 States

**SCQA.** Situation. Pressures, and natural processes, change the condition of air, water, soil and living systems. Complication. State claims were mixed with process explanations owned by EA, CH and BI, and with impacts. A concentration is a state; the harm it does is an impact. Question. What is the measured condition, and against what reference? Answer. A state claim reports a measured quantity at stated sites and dates with its uncertainty. When it judges, it names the reference condition or standard.

Key line (inductive; plural noun: states; dimension: what the state describes; order: structural, from continuous condition to episodic event to stock). Members (3): Measured conditions · Natural hazards and processes · Natural resources.

- EV.O.A2.7 Measured conditions — air, freshwater, marine, soil, and biodiversity. **A measured condition is a quantity observed at stated places and times and reported with its uncertainty.**
  - EV.O.A2.1 Air quality — the measurement uses CH and EA. **Air quality is a measured concentration of a stated pollutant, averaged over a stated time, against a stated guideline.**
    - EV.O.A2.1.1 Particulate matter. Particles are reported as mass concentration below 2.5 micrometres (PM2.5) or 10 micrometres (PM10) aerodynamic diameter. Continuous monitors need calibration against reference gravimetric measurement. The exposure–response is MD.O.A2.4.
    - EV.O.A2.1.2 Averaging time and exceedance. Guideline values are tied to averaging times. A site can meet an annual mean and exceed a 24-hour value. Exceedance counts depend on monitor siting and data completeness rules.
    - EV.O.A2.1.3 Dispersion and airsheds. Concentration at a site depends on emission and weather. The Gaussian plume model gives downwind concentration. Hypotheses: steady emission rate, steady wind and uniform turbulence, flat terrain, and no reaction or deposition. A stable inversion keeps emissions near the ground (uses EA.O.A4.3).
    - EV.O.A2.1.4 Source apportionment. Receptor models estimate source shares of ambient particles from measured composition. Hypotheses: mass is conserved between source and receptor, source profiles are stable, and the mix is linear. Collinear sources cannot be separated.
  - EV.O.A2.2 Freshwater quality — nitrate, E. coli, sediment. **Freshwater quality is a set of measured concentrations and indicators in a stated water body, compared with a numeric attribute or guideline.**
    - EV.O.A2.2.1 Nutrients and algal response. Nitrogen and phosphorus concentrations are states. Algal biomass, measured as chlorophyll a, is the response state. Liebig's law of the minimum holds when one resource limits growth at a time. Co-limitation is also documented, so the limiting nutrient is a claim for each site.
    - EV.O.A2.2.2 Faecal indicators. Escherichia coli is a faecal indicator. It shows faecal contamination, not the presence of a named pathogen. The link from concentration to illness risk is epidemiological (MD.O.A5.1).
    - EV.O.A2.2.3 Oxygen and organic load. Five-day biochemical oxygen demand measures degradable load. Hypotheses: incubation at 20 degrees Celsius in the dark, a suitable microbial seed, and no toxic inhibition. The Streeter–Phelps model gives the oxygen sag below a discharge. Hypotheses: steady flow, a single point source, and first-order decay and reaeration (uses CH.O.A4.1).
    - EV.O.A2.2.4 Sediment and clarity. Suspended sediment is mass per volume. Turbidity and visual clarity are optical proxies that need site-specific calibration to mass. Sediment source and transport are EA.O.A4.1.
  - EV.O.A2.3 Marine environment — acidification, warming. **The marine state is a measured condition of seawater, seabed or coast at stated places and depths, reported against a stated baseline.**
    - EV.O.A2.3.1 Acidification. Seawater pH falls as the ocean takes up carbon dioxide. Carbonate chemistry links dissolved inorganic carbon, alkalinity and pH (uses CH.O.A3.4). The decline since the pre-industrial period is of the order of 0.1 pH units (UNVERIFIED figure).
    - EV.O.A2.3.2 Warming and marine heatwaves. Sea surface temperature is reported as an anomaly against a stated baseline period. A marine heatwave is defined by exceeding a percentile threshold for a minimum duration (W12). Each study must state both. The ocean state is EA.O.A4.3.
    - EV.O.A2.3.3 Coastal habitat extent. Extent of seagrass, mangrove and kelp is reported as area with mapping accuracy. Loss of extent is a state. Its cause is a pressure claim (EV.O.A1.2) or an attribution claim (EV.O.A3.3).
    - EV.O.A2.3.4 Marine debris. Debris is reported as items or mass per area or volume. Microplastic is conventionally particles below 5 mm (W12). Counts depend on mesh size and method, so counts from different methods are not comparable.
  - EV.O.A2.4 Soil state — contamination, erosion. **Soil state is a measured property of a soil profile at a stated depth and date, compared with a stated reference or limit.** The soil body is EA.O.A4.5.
    - EV.O.A2.4.1 Contaminant concentration and availability. A total concentration overstates risk where the contaminant is tightly bound. The bioavailable fraction depends on soil chemistry (uses CH). Guideline values apply to a stated land use.
    - EV.O.A2.4.2 Erosion rate. The Universal Soil Loss Equation multiplies rainfall, soil, slope, cover and practice factors. Hypotheses: sheet and rill erosion only, a long-term average, and factors calibrated for the region. Gully erosion and landslides are outside it. The process is EA.O.A4.1.
    - EV.O.A2.4.3 Soil organic carbon stock. A stock is concentration times bulk density times depth. When bulk density changes, a change in stock is compared at equivalent soil mass, not at fixed depth. Detecting change needs repeat sampling by one protocol.
  - EV.O.A2.5 Biodiversity status — threat classifications (IUCN Red List criteria, W12). **Biodiversity status is a measured or assessed condition of a species, population or community, stated under a named scheme.**
    - EV.O.A2.5.1 Threat categories. The IUCN Red List assigns a species to a category from Extinct to Least Concern by rules applied to estimates. Five criteria, A to E, assign the three threatened categories. Data Deficient and Not Evaluated sit outside the threat scale. National schemes differ, so a sentence names its scheme.
    - EV.O.A2.5.2 Abundance and detection. Counts are biased low where detection probability is below one. Occupancy models estimate detection from repeat visits. Hypotheses: the site is closed to change between visits, detections are independent, and there are no false positives.
    - EV.O.A2.5.3 Richness and diversity indices. Richness is a count that depends on sampling effort. The Shannon index combines richness and evenness. Hypotheses: individuals are sampled at random, and every species present is represented. The species–area relation is a power law for areas of similar habitat and comparable effort (uses BI.O.A5.3).
    - EV.O.A2.5.4 Extinction declarations. A declaration of extinction needs surveys adequate to detect the species if it were present. Declarations are revisable because some species are rediscovered. A possibly-extinct tag records unresolved doubt. Loss of a species as harm is EV.O.A3.1.
  - EV.O.A2.7.1 Reference condition. A condition is judged against a reference. The reference may be a pre-impact record, a least-disturbed site or a modelled expectation. The choice changes the classification of a site. A reference taken from a recently degraded state understates change.
  - EV.O.A2.7.2 Composite indices. An index combines several variables by stated weights and an aggregation rule. Two indices of one water body can rank sites differently. Hypotheses: weights and rule are stated before use. The index is W12 over W4 inputs.
  - EV.O.A2.7.3 Trend detection. A trend claim needs a record long against its variability. The seasonal Kendall test detects a monotonic trend. Hypotheses: seasonal adjustment, and independent observations after adjustment. Serial correlation inflates significance (uses MA.O.A5).
- EV.O.A2.6 Natural hazards and processes — geological, hydro-meteorological, and biogeochemical events. **A hazard event is stated by its magnitude, place and date, not by the harm it does.** An earthquake or a volcanic eruption is a state, not an impact. Flood, storm, drought, heat wave, and natural disaster are hydro-meteorological hazards.
  - EV.O.A2.6.1 Geological events. An earthquake is reported by moment magnitude, a logarithmic scale of seismic moment, and by location. An eruption is reported by size and style. The process is EA.O.A2. CONTESTED: EA.O.A2.5 routes an eruption as a hazard event to EA.O.A5.5.
  - EV.O.A2.6.2 Hydro-meteorological events and return periods. An event is defined by exceeding a threshold. A return period T means an annual exceedance probability of 1/T. Extreme value analysis fits annual maxima. Hypotheses: independent maxima, a stationary record, and a record long against the return period sought. Non-stationarity defeats it (uses EA.O.A4.2).
  - EV.O.A2.6.3 Wildfire. A wildfire is reported by area burned and intensity. A fire weather index states the condition of atmosphere and fuel. A human ignition is a pressure (EV.O.A1); the burned area is the state.
  - EV.O.A2.6.4 Biogeochemical episodes. Harmful algal blooms, hypoxic events and acid pulses are episodes of changed chemistry or biomass. Each is defined by a threshold in a measured variable. The mechanism is BI.O.A5.4 or EA.O.A4.2. The harm is EV.O.A3.1.
- EV.O.A2.9 Natural resources — stocks of water, minerals, energy, and living systems. **A natural resource is a stock stated by quantity, quality and renewal rate.**
  - EV.O.A2.9.1 Stock and renewal. A renewable stock is replaced at a rate. A non-renewable stock is not replaced on human time scales. A claim states both the stock and the rate. A drainage-basin water balance P = ET + Q + ΔS closes the account (uses EA.O.A4.2).
  - EV.O.A2.9.2 Resources and reserves. A mineral resource is a geological estimate. A reserve is the part recoverable under stated prices, technology and law. Reserve figures change with price. The geology is EA.O.A1.5; the price is EC.
  - EV.O.A2.9.3 Status of harvested stocks. Status is biomass relative to a reference point. Reference points come from a stock model, so status depends on that model's hypotheses. The production model is under EV.O.A1.3 (uses BI.O.A5.1).
  - EV.O.A2.9.4 Natural capital accounts. Accounts record stocks and their changes in physical units. The System of Environmental-Economic Accounting is a statistical standard (W12). Monetary valuation of a stock is EC. The accounting method is EV.M.5.

Environmental concepts are not a measured state. A name for the field, a culture, or a teaching term is routed to its own field. Earth-system sciences are EA. Both placements are retired in the registry and the ids are not reused.

Named objects: PM2.5 and PM10, Gaussian plume model (steady emission rate, steady wind and uniform turbulence, flat terrain, no reaction or deposition), receptor model (mass conserved, stable source profiles, linear mixing), Liebig's law of the minimum (one limiting resource at a time), five-day biochemical oxygen demand test (20 degrees Celsius, dark, seeded, no inhibition), Streeter–Phelps oxygen sag model (steady flow, single point source, first-order decay and reaeration), Universal Soil Loss Equation (sheet and rill erosion, long-term average, regional calibration), IUCN Red List categories and criteria A to E, occupancy model (closure, independent detections, no false positives), Shannon index (random sample, all species represented), species–area relation (similar habitat, comparable effort), seasonal Kendall test (seasonal adjustment, independent observations), extreme value analysis and return period (independent maxima, stationary record, long record), reference condition.

### EV.O.A3 Impacts

**SCQA.** Situation. A changed state harms ecosystems and people. Complication. Harm needs a receptor, an exposure and a causal link. v1 held the physical model, the exposure–response and the valuation in one cell. Attribution is both a claim about shares of a pressure and a claim about a physical model. Question. What harm, to which receptor, attributed how? Answer. An impact claim names a receptor, a measured change in it, and the warrant that links the change to the pressure (W5 or W3). A correlation alone does not meet it. Risk is hazard times exposure times vulnerability (uses EA.O.A5.5); the hazard is EV.O.A2.6 and the rest is here.

Key line (inductive; plural noun: impact claims; dimension: the object of the harm claim; order: structural, from ecosystem to person to link to benefit). Members (4): Ecosystem harm · Human health impact · Attribution · Ecosystem services.

- EV.O.A3.1 Ecosystem harm — loss of function and species. **Ecosystem harm is a measured loss of function, composition or abundance at a stated site, attributed to a stated pressure.**
  - EV.O.A3.1.1 Toxic effect and species sensitivity. A species sensitivity distribution fits toxicity endpoints across species. The concentration hazardous to 5 percent of species (HC5) is read from it. Hypotheses: tested species are a random sample of the community, endpoints are comparable, and the distribution shape fits the data.
  - EV.O.A3.1.2 Eutrophication effects. Enrichment raises algal biomass, which lowers dissolved oxygen as it decays. Each link in the chain is stated and measured. Recovery can lag after nutrient reduction because sediments release stored nutrients (uses BI.O.A5.4).
  - EV.O.A3.1.3 Extinction debt. Extinction debt is the delayed loss of species after habitat loss. The claim is that present richness exceeds the equilibrium for the remaining area. Its size in real systems is OPEN (uses BI.O.A5.5).
  - EV.O.A3.1.4 Critical loads. A critical load is the highest deposition that causes no harmful long-term effect. Hypotheses: a steady-state mass balance, and a known chemical threshold for harm. Exceedance is deposition minus critical load (uses CH.O.A3.4).
- EV.O.A3.2 Human health impact — the exposure–response is MD. **A health impact is a change in disease, injury or death in a population caused by an environmental exposure.**
  - EV.O.A3.2.1 Exposure assessment. Exposure is contact with an agent through a pathway, not the ambient concentration. Personal exposure differs from a fixed-site measurement. Using an area average for individuals can bias the exposure–response estimate (uses MD.O.A5.1).
  - EV.O.A3.2.2 Reference doses. A reference dose is derived from tested doses with uncertainty factors, through a no-observed-adverse-effect level or a benchmark dose. Hypotheses: a threshold exists for the effect, the critical effect is identified, and the factors cover differences between species and between people (uses MD.O.A2.4).
  - EV.O.A3.2.3 Low-dose radiation. The linear no-threshold model takes risk as proportional to dose with no safe threshold, for stochastic effects such as cancer. It is a protection assumption at low dose and is CONTESTED there.
  - EV.O.A3.2.4 Attributable fraction of illness. The population attributable fraction is p(RR − 1) / (1 + p(RR − 1)), with p the exposure prevalence and RR the relative risk. Hypotheses: the exposure causes the illness, the relative risk applies to this population, and confounding is controlled (uses MD.O.A5.1).
- EV.O.A3.3 Attribution — the share of an observed change caused by a stated pressure (e.g. event attribution in climate science). **An attribution claim states a share of change, its counterfactual and its uncertainty.** The physical model is EA.
  - EV.O.A3.3.1 Detection and attribution. Detection shows that a change exceeds internal variability. Attribution assigns the change to a cause against a counterfactual without that cause. A detected change is not thereby attributed (uses EA.O.A4.3).
  - EV.O.A3.3.2 Event attribution. Event attribution compares the probability of an event class with the pressure (P1) and without it (P0). The fraction of attributable risk is 1 − P0/P1. Hypotheses: models represent the event class, and the event definition is fixed before analysis. Results depend on that definition (uses EA.O.A4.3).
  - EV.O.A3.3.3 Ecological attribution by design. A before–after–control–impact design attributes an ecological change to an activity. Hypotheses: control sites resemble the affected site, sampling occurs before and after at both, and controls are not affected (EV.M.2).
  - EV.O.A3.3.4 Interacting pressures. Where pressures interact, shares are not additive. Single-pressure attributions can sum to more or less than 100 percent. A claim states whether it assigns shares or marginal effects.
- EV.O.A3.4 Ecosystem services — benefits people receive; valuation is EC. **An ecosystem service is a benefit stated by its ecosystem function, its beneficiary and its unit.**
  - EV.O.A3.4.1 Service classification. The Millennium Ecosystem Assessment groups services as provisioning, regulating, cultural and supporting. Later schemes separate final from intermediate services. Adding supporting and final services double counts. The scheme is a convention (W12).
  - EV.O.A3.4.2 Biophysical supply. A service is first a measured flow, such as water purified, carbon stored or crop pollinated. A production function links ecosystem state to the flow. Supply is not value, and a flow with no beneficiary is not a benefit.
  - EV.O.A3.4.3 Valuation methods. Valuation is owned by EC. Contingent valuation asks what people would pay for a described change. Hypotheses: respondents understand the change, hypothetical bias is controlled, and the sample represents those affected. Cost–benefit analysis needs effects valued in money and a stated discount rate (uses EC.O.A1.4, EC.O.A5.3).
  - EV.O.A3.4.4 Trade-offs between services. Services covary. Raising one, such as crop yield, often lowers another, such as water quality. A trade-off claim needs measured services at the same sites and period (uses AG.O.A5.3).

Named objects: species sensitivity distribution and HC5 (random sample of species, comparable endpoints, adequate distribution fit), critical load (steady-state mass balance, known chemical threshold), no-observed-adverse-effect level and benchmark dose (threshold exists, critical effect identified, uncertainty factors cover variation), linear no-threshold model (stochastic effects; CONTESTED at low dose), population attributable fraction (causal exposure, applicable relative risk, confounding controlled), fraction of attributable risk (event definition fixed, models represent the event class), detection and attribution, extinction debt (OPEN), contingent valuation and cost–benefit analysis (uses EC), ecosystem service.

### EV.O.A4 Responses

**SCQA.** Situation. Societies act to cut pressures, restore states or limit harm. Complication. A response claim mixes the intention, the instrument and the measured effect. Policy, price and duty belong to PO, EC and LA. Question. Which responses change a pressure or a state, and how is the change shown? Answer. EV owns the measured environmental effect of a response and the design that identifies it. The instrument is owned by PO, EC or LA. The effect is a W3 or W5 claim, not a statement of intent.

Key line (inductive; plural noun: responses; dimension: what the response acts on; order: structural, from ecosystem to place to emitter to works to practice). Members (5): Restoration · Conservation · Regulation · Environmental engineering · Kaitiakitanga.

- EV.O.A4.1 Restoration — measured recovery after intervention. **A restoration claim states a measured recovery toward a reference after an intervention, with a control that separates it from natural recovery.**
  - EV.O.A4.1.1 Recovery trajectories. Recovery is measured against a reference state. Function, composition and structure recover at different rates, and a claim names which. A system with alternative stable states may not return along its path of loss (EV.O.A5.1).
  - EV.O.A4.1.2 Active and passive restoration. Active restoration, such as planting, and passive restoration, by removing the pressure, are different interventions. A claim that active work is needed must be tested against a passive control.
  - EV.O.A4.1.3 Success criteria. Success is judged against targets set before the work. A monitoring period shorter than the recovery time can give a false failure or a false success. Reference sites must share the physical setting.
  - EV.O.A4.1.4 Bioremediation. Environmental biotechnology uses organisms to remove or transform a contaminant. Rate is often modelled as first-order or Monod kinetics. Hypotheses: degraders are present, an electron acceptor is available, and the contaminant is bioavailable (uses BI.O.A2.3).
- EV.O.A4.2 Conservation — protected areas, predator control (Predator Free 2050 is a stated goal, UNVERIFIED name and date). **A conservation claim states the effect of a protected area, species programme or pest control on a measured population or habitat, against a counterfactual.**
  - EV.O.A4.2.1 Protected area effect. The effect is the difference between protected land and matched unprotected land. Protected areas are often sited where clearance was unlikely, so an unmatched comparison overstates the effect.
  - EV.O.A4.2.2 Systematic conservation planning. The method selects complementary sites that meet quantitative targets at least cost. Hypotheses: distribution data exist for the features, targets are set, and site costs are known. Complementarity means a site's value depends on the sites already chosen.
  - EV.O.A4.2.3 Eradication and suppression. Eradication is feasible only if every individual is at risk, removal exceeds replacement at all densities, and reinvasion is prevented. Suppression to a threshold density is a different goal (uses EV.O.A1.4).
  - EV.O.A4.2.4 Translocation and viability analysis. A translocation succeeds when a self-sustaining population results after a stated period. Population viability analysis estimates extinction risk from demographic rates. Hypotheses: rates are estimated without large error, and stochasticity and catastrophes are modelled. Its absolute probabilities are CONTESTED; rankings of options are more robust (uses BI.O.A5.1).
- EV.O.A4.3 Regulation and instruments — the policy is PO, the price instrument EC, the duty LA. **EV owns the measured environmental effect.**
  - EV.O.A4.3.1 Compliance and outcome. A rule's compliance rate is not an environmental outcome. The claim must show a change in a pressure or a state attributable to the rule. That needs a counterfactual (W5).
  - EV.O.A4.3.2 Caps and leakage. A cap is judged by emissions under it against a counterfactual path. Leakage is displacement of emissions to uncovered places or periods. Market design is EC (uses EC.O.A5.3). The political cause is PO.O.A4.4.
  - EV.O.A4.3.3 Impact assessment as procedure. Environmental impact assessment predicts the effects of a proposal and its alternatives. Hypotheses: the proposal and alternatives are defined, and baseline conditions are surveyed. A prediction audit compares each predicted effect with later monitoring. The legal requirement is LA.O.A1.1.
  - EV.O.A4.3.4 Treaties and controlled substances. The effect of a treaty is the change in a pressure it controls. The Montreal Protocol's control of ozone-depleting substances is a cited case, and its production record needs a source (UNVERIFIED). Recovery of the ozone state is a separate, later claim. Legal force is LA.O.A1.4; the institution is PO.O.A5.2.
- EV.O.A4.7 Environmental engineering — technical work that changes a pressure or a state. **An engineering response is a designed works or process, judged by measured performance against a specification.** Measurement, monitoring, and assessment are methods (EV.M.1 and EV.M.2), not a response cell.
  - EV.O.A4.7.1 Treatment performance. A treatment works is judged by removal efficiency, (inflow − outflow) / inflow, over a stated period. Efficiency varies with load and temperature. The reaction kinetics are CH.O.A4.1; the unit operation is EN.
  - EV.O.A4.7.2 Comparing options by life cycle. Life cycle assessment totals burdens from raw material to disposal (ISO 14040). Hypotheses: a functional unit is defined, the boundary is stated, and inventory data represent the processes. Burden shifting between impact categories is its main defeater (EV.M.5).
  - EV.O.A4.7.3 Stormwater and flood works. A works is designed to a return period, which states its annual exceedance probability. It does not protect against larger events. The design event comes from EV.O.A2.6 and the hydraulics from EN.O.A2.3.
  - EV.O.A4.7.4 Waste flows. A waste system is described by a material flow analysis of tonnes in, recovered and disposed. A recycling rate must state whether it counts mass collected or mass reprocessed. The waste hierarchy is a heuristic, not evidence of lower burden.
- EV.O.A4.4 Kaitiakitanga in practice — guardianship as a response; its tradition-internal meaning is IK. No child cells are added here: IK content is not placed in this file pending Māori-led review (Standard v3).

Named objects: restoration trajectory and reference state, bioremediation (degraders present, electron acceptor available, contaminant bioavailable), protected area effect (matched comparison), systematic conservation planning (distribution data, set targets, known costs), eradication criteria, population viability analysis (rates well estimated, stochasticity and catastrophes modelled; CONTESTED for absolute risk), environmental impact assessment (defined proposal and alternatives, surveyed baseline), leakage, removal efficiency, life cycle assessment (functional unit, stated boundary, representative inventory), design return period, adaptive management (outcomes monitored, managers able to change course, competing hypotheses stated).

### EV.O.A5 Limits

**SCQA.** Situation. Some values of a state or a pressure are treated as limits, set by a system property, by agreement or by law. Complication. A limit is partly a threshold in a system and partly a chosen value, and the two are often reported as one number. Question. Where are the limits, and what kind of claim sets each? Answer. A limit claim states what fixes the value. It is a property of the system (a threshold), a choice (a standard) or a synthesis (a capacity or boundary). It names the warrant for that kind.

Key line (inductive; plural noun: limits; dimension: what fixes the value; order: structural, from system property to chosen value to composite). Members (3): Thresholds and tipping points · Standards and bottom lines · Carrying capacity and planetary boundaries.

- EV.O.A5.1 Thresholds and tipping points — a regime shift occurs past a critical value, assuming bistable dynamics (theory MA.O.A4). **A threshold claim says that a driver has a critical value past which the system moves to another regime, under stated dynamics.**
  - EV.O.A5.1.1 Alternative stable states. A regime shift needs two stable states over a range of the driver. Shallow lakes are a proposed case, clear or turbid. Demonstrations in field systems are few, and general bistability in ecosystems is CONTESTED (uses MA.O.A4).
  - EV.O.A5.1.2 Hysteresis. Under bistability the threshold for return differs from the threshold for loss. Lowering a driver to its earlier value does not restore the earlier state. The test is to move the driver up and then down.
  - EV.O.A5.1.3 Early warning signals. Rising variance and autocorrelation precede a transition in models approaching a fold bifurcation. Whether such signals work in real systems is OPEN (uses MA.O.A4).
  - EV.O.A5.1.4 Climate tipping elements. Parts of the ice sheets and ocean circulation are proposed as tipping elements. Threshold values are model-dependent and OPEN (uses EA.O.A4.4, EA.O.A4.3).
- EV.O.A5.2 Standards and bottom lines — legal or agreed limits (W12; force LA). **A standard is a value fixed by agreement or law; its scientific basis and its legal force are separate claims.**
  - EV.O.A5.2.1 Health-based standards. A health guideline is derived from the exposure–response with uncertainty factors. A guideline is advisory; a standard is binding in a jurisdiction (force LA). Where no threshold has been identified, as for fine particles, the value is a chosen level, not a threshold.
  - EV.O.A5.2.2 Ecosystem-based standards. A protective concentration can be derived from a species sensitivity distribution (EV.O.A3.1.1). The percentage of species to protect is a policy choice. The derivation holds only under that method's hypotheses.
  - EV.O.A5.2.3 Numeric bottom lines. A bottom line is an attribute value below which a water body is not to fall. It is set by a stated process and tested by monitoring. The NZ freshwater instrument that names national bottom lines is UNVERIFIED in detail.
  - EV.O.A5.2.4 Compliance statistics. A standard stated as a percentile or a mean needs a sampling frequency adequate to estimate it. A 95th-percentile standard estimated from few samples has wide error. Sampling error can decide compliance (uses MA.O.A5).
- EV.O.A5.3 Carrying capacity and planetary boundaries — the planetary boundaries framework (Rockström et al., 2009) is a proposed set, CONTESTED in its numbers. **A capacity or boundary claim aggregates thresholds and flows into one limit for a stated system and period.**
  - EV.O.A5.3.1 Carrying capacity. K in the logistic model is a fixed capacity under stated conditions (uses BI.O.A5.1). A human carrying capacity is not fixed, because technology, consumption and trade change it. Human capacity figures are CONTESTED unless their assumptions are stated.
  - EV.O.A5.3.2 The nine boundary processes. The 2009 proposal has nine processes: climate change, ocean acidification, stratospheric ozone, nitrogen and phosphorus flows, freshwater use, land-system change, biodiversity loss, aerosol loading and chemical pollution. Two of the nine lacked a quantified boundary in 2009 (UNVERIFIED which). Each has a control variable and a proposed value.
  - EV.O.A5.3.3 Boundary and uncertainty. A boundary is proposed on the safe side of the uncertainty around a threshold. It is a normative choice built on a scientific estimate. Interaction between boundaries is OPEN.
  - EV.O.A5.3.4 Catchment and regional limits. A global boundary gives no catchment limit. A catchment load limit is the load at which a response variable meets its target, read from a load–response relationship. The target is a standard (EV.O.A5.2). Allocation among users is normative.

Named objects: bistability and hysteresis (two stable states over a driver range), critical slowing down (approach to a fold bifurcation; OPEN in real systems), standard and guideline value, bottom line, percentile compliance, carrying capacity K (logistic growth, fixed resources), planetary boundaries (Rockström et al., 2009; CONTESTED in numbers), safe operating space, load–response relationship.
