# HI — History

Standard v2 cited, not copied. Built up from prototype_history_language.md; its members are kept. Dates the prototype gave from memory are marked UNVERIFIED unless widely attested.

## 1. SCQA
**Situation.** History states what happened in the human past, when, why, what changed, and what it meant. Its claims rest on records that survive.
**Complication.** Every other pyramid cites past events: the strategist's case, the economist's crisis, the mathematician's life. v1 had no owner for the dated fact, so each field certified its own.
**Question.** What partition places every claim about the human past once, so that other pyramids can cite one owner of the dated fact?
**Answer.** Place a claim by what it asserts about the past: occurrence, time, cause, change and continuity, or meaning and memory. Every cell owes W7, source criticism of a dated record.

## 2. Governing thought
History is exhausted by four faces of one pyramid: objects, methods, warrants and bridges.
Its objects divide into five assertions about the past: occurrence, time, cause, change and continuity, and meaning and memory.

## 3. Key line
Dimension: **what the sentence asserts about the human past.** Order: from the single fact to its meaning.
1. Occurrence
2. Time
3. Cause
4. Change and continuity
5. Meaning and memory

A period, region or nation is a catalogue (substrate), not a cell. "Medieval Europe" indexes claims; it does not hold them.

## 4. Face O — Objects

Each A-cell below states a fuller SCQA and a key line. The first-level sub-cells keep their IDs and meaning. Child cells one level down state testable claims. Dates given from memory remain UNVERIFIED unless widely attested.

### HI.O.A1 Occurrence
**SCQA.** The base claim of history is that something happened. Records lie, decay and conflict. What makes "this happened" acceptable? Key line: the event, the actor, the place, the authenticity of the record.

Situation. A historian asserts that a named event took place, involving named people, at a named place. Complication. The record that carries the assertion may be late, partisan, copied, damaged or forged. Survival is uneven, so silence is weak evidence of absence. Question. Which claims about occurrence does history own, and what must each show? Answer. History owns the event as a unit of record, the actor, the human presence at a place, and the authenticity of the record. Each owes W7.

Key line (inductive; plural noun: occurrence claims; dimension: which element of "this happened" the claim fixes; order: what, who, where, then on what record). Members (4): The event · The actor · The place · Authenticity of the record.

- HI.O.A1.1 The event — the unit of record, separated from its interpretation (the fact–interpretation distinction, E. H. Carr, *What Is History?*, 1961).
  **An event claim asserts a bounded happening that a record could confirm or refute, apart from any judgement of its significance.**
  - HI.O.A1.1.1 **Event boundaries are stipulated.** Where an event begins and ends is set by the historian's description. Two descriptions of one happening can count as different events.
  - HI.O.A1.1.2 **Selection is not fabrication.** Carr argued that facts become historical facts by selection. Selection affects significance, not whether the happening occurred. CONTESTED: some theorists hold that description makes the fact.
  - HI.O.A1.1.3 **An argument from silence needs an expected record.** The absence of an event in sources supports non-occurrence only where a surviving record would be expected to mention it.
- HI.O.A1.2 The actor — persons and groups; prosopography (collective biography).
  **An actor claim identifies a person or group as present or acting, by a name or description that links distinct records.**
  - HI.O.A1.2.1 **Identity across records is a linkage claim.** Two records name the same person only if name, date, place and relationships fit. Homonyms are the standard defeater.
  - HI.O.A1.2.2 **Prosopography infers group patterns from many careers.** It holds when the group is defined in advance and comparable records exist for many members. Elites are usually over-recorded.
  - HI.O.A1.2.3 **A collective actor is a shorthand.** "The Crown" or "the peasants" acted only through persons. A claim about a collective must say which persons or institutions are meant.
- HI.O.A1.3 The place — located with EA tools; the claim of human presence is HI.
  **A place claim asserts that people were present or acted at a location identified in the record.**
  - HI.O.A1.3.1 **Historical place names need identification.** A name in a record must be matched to a location. Names move, split and repeat, so the match is an argument.
  - HI.O.A1.3.2 **The coordinates are borrowed.** Survey, mapping and geographic analysis are owned by Earth science and information work (uses EA.O.A5). History owns only the presence claim.
- HI.O.A1.4 Authenticity of the record — forgery and interpolation. Lorenzo Valla showed the Donation of Constantine to be a forgery (c. 1440) by philology.
  **An authenticity claim asserts that a record is what it purports to be in origin, date, author and integrity.**
  - HI.O.A1.4.1 **Anachronism defeats a claimed date.** A word, form, title or material that did not exist at the claimed date shows a later origin. Valla's case used anachronistic Latin (uses LN.O.A5).
  - HI.O.A1.4.2 **Diplomatics tests a document by its form.** It holds where dated authentic documents of the same kind and chancery survive for comparison.
  - HI.O.A1.4.3 **Interpolation is local forgery.** A genuine record can contain added passages. Authenticity is therefore judged passage by passage, not only document by document.
  - HI.O.A1.4.4 **Provenance is evidence of authenticity, not proof.** An unbroken chain of custody lowers the risk of forgery. A gap in the chain raises it but does not settle it.

Named objects: the historical fact, the event, the argument from silence, prosopography, diplomatics, provenance, interpolation, anachronism test, triangulation of independent records. Method hypotheses: source criticism (the record's origin can be investigated; independent records exist to compare); diplomatics (dated authentic documents of the same kind exist); prosopography (the group is defined; comparable records exist for many members); triangulation (the records do not derive from one another).

### HI.O.A2 Time
**SCQA.** A fact must be placed in time to be used. Records use different calendars and clocks. When did it happen? Key line: absolute date, relative order, synchronism, periodisation.

Situation. Every use of a past fact needs its date or its place in a sequence. Complication. Records count time by regnal years, eras, festivals and local calendars. Reforms took effect on different dates in different places. Physical dating methods give ranges, not days. Question. Which claims about time does history own? Answer. History owns the date of a human event, its order relative to others, the alignment of records, and the period labels used. It cites physical dates from EA and PH.

Key line (inductive; plural noun: temporal claims; dimension: how the claim fixes time; order: from a point, to a sequence, to an alignment, to a label, to a borrowed measurement). Members (5): Absolute date · Relative order · Synchronism · Periodisation · Scientific dating.

- HI.O.A2.1 Absolute date — calendars and eras: Julian, Gregorian (introduced 1582), AH, BCE/CE.
  **An absolute date claim places an event on a calendar with stated rules and a stated starting point.**
  - HI.O.A2.1.1 **Calendar conversion holds only with known rules.** A date converts correctly when both calendars' rules and epochs are known, and the local date of any reform.
  - HI.O.A2.1.2 **Reform dates differ by place.** The Gregorian calendar was adopted at different dates in different states. A date between adoptions must state its style, Old Style or New Style.
  - HI.O.A2.1.3 **The start of the year varies.** Some calendars began the year on a date other than 1 January. A date early in the year can be one year out without this check.
- HI.O.A2.2 Relative order — terminus post quem and ante quem.
  **A relative-order claim places an event before or after another, without needing a calendar date.**
  - HI.O.A2.2.1 **A latest dated element sets the earliest date.** A deposit or document can be no earlier than its latest datable part. Hoard dating by the latest coin is the standard case.
  - HI.O.A2.2.2 **Stratigraphy orders deposits.** Lower layers are earlier where deposits are undisturbed. Pits, slumping and reworking defeat the order (uses EA.O.A3).
  - HI.O.A2.2.3 **Seriation orders assemblages by style.** It holds where styles rise and fall gradually, within one tradition, over similar time spans.
- HI.O.A2.3 Synchronism — aligning independent records of the same moment.
  **A synchronism claim asserts that records from separate systems of reckoning refer to the same moment.**
  - HI.O.A2.3.1 **Shared datable phenomena anchor alignment.** An eclipse or other datable sky event in two records can tie their chronologies. The identification of the event is itself an inference.
  - HI.O.A2.3.2 **King lists are not independent clocks.** Lists can merge co-regencies or duplicate reigns. Summing reign lengths can over- or under-state elapsed time.
- HI.O.A2.4 Periodisation — labels such as "the Renaissance" are conventions (W12), not discoveries.
  **A period label is a convention for grouping claims, and a claim of its boundary is a choice to be justified.**
  - HI.O.A2.4.1 **Period boundaries vary by region and theme.** A period can begin at different dates for politics, art and economy. A sentence must say which boundary it uses.
  - HI.O.A2.4.2 **Labels carry judgements.** Terms such as "Dark Ages" import an evaluation. The evaluative content is HI.O.A5.4, not a fact of A2. CONTESTED: whether any label is neutral.
- HI.O.A2.5 Scientific dating — radiocarbon and dendrochronology dates are measurements owned by EA and PH; HI uses them.
  **A scientific date for a human event is a measured range transferred to the event by an argument about association.**
  - HI.O.A2.5.1 **Radiocarbon dates are calibrated ranges.** They hold for a closed system since death, with calibration applied, within about 50,000 years. The measurement is owned elsewhere (uses EA.O.A3).
  - HI.O.A2.5.2 **The sample dates the organism, not the event.** Old wood or reused timber gives a date earlier than the human use. The association argument is HI.
  - HI.O.A2.5.3 **Dendrochronology gives a felling date only with bark edge.** Without the outer rings, it gives an earliest possible felling date (UNVERIFIED as a universal rule; sapwood estimates are regional).

Named objects: Julian calendar, Gregorian calendar, Old Style and New Style, terminus post quem, terminus ante quem, stratigraphy, seriation, Harris matrix, synchronism, period label, calibrated radiocarbon date. Method hypotheses: calendar conversion (each calendar's rules and epoch known; local reform date known); stratigraphy (deposits undisturbed); seriation (gradual style change; one tradition; similar spans); radiocarbon (closed system; calibration; within about 50,000 years); hoard dating (one closed deposit); Harris matrix (each context's physical relations recorded during excavation).

### HI.O.A3 Cause
**SCQA.** History explains as well as records. Explanations of the past cannot be tested by re-running it. What makes a causal claim about the past acceptable? Key line: reasons of actors, conditions and structures, contingency, counterfactuals.

Situation. Historians explain why events happened and why outcomes took the form they did. Complication. The past cannot be re-run, records of motive are partial, and later knowledge invites hindsight. Explanations by economic or political models compete with narrative explanation. Question. Which causal claims about the past does history own, and what tests apply? Answer. History owns explanations by reasons, by conditions and structures, by contingency, and by constrained counterfactuals. A model-explained path belongs to the model's owner.

Key line (inductive; plural noun: causal claims; dimension: what is said to do the causing; order: from individual reasons, to structures, to chance, to the counterfactual test, to borrowed models). Members (5): Reasons of actors · Conditions and structures · Contingency · Counterfactuals · Model-explained paths.

- HI.O.A3.1 Reasons of actors — Collingwood's re-enactment of thought (*The Idea of History*, 1946).
  **A reasons claim explains an action by the beliefs and aims the actor held, as shown in the record.**
  - HI.O.A3.1.1 **Stated reasons are evidence, not proof.** An actor's stated motive can be strategic. It is tested against what the actor did and wrote privately.
  - HI.O.A3.1.2 **Reasons must be available at the time.** An explanation fails if it credits the actor with knowledge they could not have had. This is the hindsight test.
  - HI.O.A3.1.3 **Re-enactment is a method of interpretation.** Collingwood's claim that history is the re-enactment of past thought is CONTESTED as a general account; it is not a warrant on its own.
- HI.O.A3.2 Conditions and structures — necessary and sufficient conditions; structural causes.
  **A structural claim explains an outcome by conditions that constrained many actors, independent of any one actor's aims.**
  - HI.O.A3.2.1 **A necessary condition claim must survive comparison.** If the outcome occurred where the condition was absent, the condition was not necessary.
  - HI.O.A3.2.2 **Structural and intentional explanations can combine.** A structure sets options and actors choose among them. A claim must say which part it explains.
  - HI.O.A3.2.3 **Multiple causes need weighting reasons.** Ranking causes needs a stated criterion, such as counterfactual difference or timing. A list is not an explanation.
- HI.O.A3.3 Contingency and chance.
  **A contingency claim asserts that an outcome turned on an event that was not determined by prior conditions as recorded.**
  - HI.O.A3.3.1 **Contingency is relative to a description.** An event is contingent with respect to a stated set of conditions, not absolutely.
  - HI.O.A3.3.2 **Contingency claims need a plausible alternative.** To say an outcome was contingent, the historian must show a realistic alternative existed at the time.
- HI.O.A3.4 Counterfactual claims — they owe W7 for their premises and W9 for the inference. Unconstrained counterfactuals are a heuristic.
  **A counterfactual claim asserts what would have followed from a stated change, holding other recorded conditions fixed.**
  - HI.O.A3.4.1 **Minimal rewrite constrains the antecedent.** The changed condition should be one that contemporaries thought possible, with little else altered. CONTESTED: no agreed measure of "minimal".
  - HI.O.A3.4.2 **Confidence falls with distance.** Near consequences of a counterfactual can be argued from the record. Remote consequences compound uncertainty and become a heuristic.
  - HI.O.A3.4.3 **Comparative cases discipline counterfactuals.** A case where the antecedent actually differed supplies evidence. Without one, the inference rests on W9 alone.
- HI.O.A3.5 Model-explained paths — when a growth path is explained by an economic model, the explanation is EC and HI supplies the record (cliometrics is a bridge).
  **When a formal model explains a path, the explanation is owned by the model's field, and history owns the series and its fit to the record.**
  - HI.O.A3.5.1 **Historical series carry construction error.** A price or output series built from surviving accounts carries selection and conversion error. The construction is HI.
  - HI.O.A3.5.2 **Causal identification is borrowed.** Natural-experiment designs on historical data are owned by economics (uses EC). History supplies the claim that the event occurred as described.

Named objects: re-enactment, hindsight test, necessary condition, sufficient condition, structural cause, contingency, minimal-rewrite counterfactual, cliometric series. Method hypotheses: comparative history (cases comparable on stated dimensions); counterfactual inference (antecedent possible at the time; other recorded conditions held fixed).

### HI.O.A4 Change and continuity
**SCQA.** Many claims concern spans, not moments. Key line: durations, processes, structures over time, comparison across places.

Situation. Historians describe how institutions, economies and societies changed or persisted over time. Complication. Spans are built from scattered records of uneven survival. A trend may reflect changes in record-keeping rather than in the world. Comparison across places risks treating unlike cases as alike. Question. Which claims about change over spans does history own? Answer. History owns the description of durations, of processes, of structures over time, and comparisons across places. Formal demographic and economic models are cited.

Key line (inductive; plural noun: span claims; dimension: the scale and kind of the span; order: time-scale, process, structure, then comparison). Members (4): Durations · Processes · Structures over time · Comparison across places.

- HI.O.A4.1 Durations — Braudel's three: longue durée, conjoncture and événement (*La Méditerranée*, 1949).
  **A duration claim assigns a change to a time-scale and asserts that it moved at that scale.**
  - HI.O.A4.1.1 **Time-scales are analytic choices.** Braudel's three are a scheme for sorting change, not observed layers. Other scales can be used if stated.
  - HI.O.A4.1.2 **Slow change needs long series.** A claim of change over centuries requires comparable records across the span. Short series cannot show it.
- HI.O.A4.2 Processes — industrialisation, state formation. A demographic transition is cited from MS.
  **A process claim asserts a directional sequence of changes linked by mechanism over a span.**
  - HI.O.A4.2.1 **A process name is not an explanation.** Calling a change "modernisation" groups events; it does not explain them. The mechanism must be stated (HI.O.A3).
  - HI.O.A4.2.2 **Stage theories are CONTESTED.** A claim that societies pass through fixed stages in order must survive counter-cases. Most such schemes have known exceptions.
  - HI.O.A4.2.3 **Demographic models are cited.** Population structure and transition models are owned by the population sciences (uses MS.O.A5). History supplies parish, census and tax records.
- HI.O.A4.3 Structures over time — institutions, economies, environments in human time.
  **A structure claim asserts that an institution or arrangement persisted or changed in form across a span.**
  - HI.O.A4.3.1 **Persistence needs a continuity criterion.** An institution with the same name may change function. The claim must say what stayed the same.
  - HI.O.A4.3.2 **Environmental history uses borrowed proxies.** Climate and land-cover records are owned by EA (uses EA.O.A3). The claim about human response is HI.
- HI.O.A4.4 Comparison across places — comparative history; world and global history.
  **A comparative claim asserts a similarity or difference between cases on stated dimensions, and may explain it.**
  - HI.O.A4.4.1 **Cases must be comparable on stated dimensions.** A comparison holds only where cases share the dimensions it uses, measured in compatible ways.
  - HI.O.A4.4.2 **Connection is not comparison.** Global history traces links between places. Linked cases are not independent, so they cannot serve as separate tests.
  - HI.O.A4.4.3 **Few cases limit inference.** With many variables and few cases, comparison can suggest causes but rarely isolate them (uses MA.O.A5 for any statistical claim).

Named objects: longue durée, conjoncture, événement, process, stage theory, institution, environmental history, comparative method, connected history. Method hypotheses: comparative history (cases comparable on stated dimensions); quantitative series (records comparable across the span; record-keeping change controlled for).

### HI.O.A5 Meaning and memory
**SCQA.** The past meant things to those who lived it and to those who remember it. Key line: meaning to participants, collective memory, heritage, historiography.

Situation. The past carries meanings for participants, for later communities and for later historians. Complication. Meaning is easily projected backward. Memory changes with present needs. Heritage mixes record, law and identity. Question. Which claims about meaning and memory does history own? Answer. History owns claims about what the past meant to participants, how groups remembered it, how it is commemorated, how historians interpreted it, and dated oral accounts under W7.

Key line (inductive; plural noun: meaning claims; dimension: whose meaning the claim reports; order: participants, groups who remember, public institutions, historians, oral witnesses). Members (5): Meaning to participants · Collective memory · Heritage and commemoration · Historiography · Oral history.

- HI.O.A5.1 Meaning to participants — the history of mentalités.
  **A participant-meaning claim reports what an event or practice meant to people at the time, as shown in their records.**
  - HI.O.A5.1.1 **Meaning is read from contemporary usage.** The sense of a term at the time is tested against its use in other records of the period (uses LN.O.A4).
  - HI.O.A5.1.2 **Literate records over-represent some groups.** A claim about a whole society's outlook must address who wrote the surviving records.
- HI.O.A5.2 Collective memory — Halbwachs (1925); lieux de mémoire (Nora, 1984–92).
  **A memory claim reports how a group later recalled and represented a past event, as distinct from what happened.**
  - HI.O.A5.2.1 **Memory is dated too.** A claim about how a group remembered an event must be placed in time, since memory changes.
  - HI.O.A5.2.2 **Memory is evidence of the rememberers.** A commemorative account is strong evidence of later beliefs and weak evidence of the original event.
- HI.O.A5.3 Heritage and commemoration — monuments, museums. A heritage-law duty is LA.
  **A heritage claim reports how a site, object or practice was selected, kept and presented as significant.**
  - HI.O.A5.3.1 **Significance assessments are W12 judgements.** A statement that a place is significant applies stated criteria. The criteria, not the place, carry the judgement.
  - HI.O.A5.3.2 **A monument dates its makers.** A memorial is a primary record of the time it was erected, not of the event it marks.
- HI.O.A5.4 Historiography — what later historians made of the past. Schools of interpretation are answer-families, not cells.
  **A historiographical claim reports how historians interpreted a subject, and why interpretations changed.**
  - HI.O.A5.4.1 **Interpretations are tested against shared records.** Rival schools can be compared on how well each accounts for the same sources.
  - HI.O.A5.4.2 **New sources and new questions both move interpretations.** A historiographical claim should say which drove a change.
  - HI.O.A5.4.3 **Presentism is a defeater, not a ban.** Asking present questions of the past is allowed. Attributing present concepts to past actors is the error.
- HI.O.A5.5 Oral history — a dated account owes W7 like any record. A tradition-internal account (whakapapa as authority) is IK under W13 and does not travel into A1 without W7.
  **An oral-history claim treats a recorded interview or testimony as a dated source subject to source criticism.**
  - HI.O.A5.5.1 **The interview is the record.** The date, setting and interviewer of a recorded account are part of its provenance and must be kept.
  - HI.O.A5.5.2 **Testimony is tested like documents.** Internal consistency, distance from the event and corroboration apply to oral accounts as to written ones.

Named objects: mentalités, collective memory, lieux de mémoire, heritage significance, commemoration, historiography, presentism, oral-history interview. Method hypotheses: source criticism of testimony (the account's origin and setting can be investigated; independent records exist to compare).

## 5. Face M — Methods

| ID | Role | Shared role | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| HI.M.1 | Find and authenticate | M3 Observe | archives, palaeography, diplomatics |
| HI.M.2 | Date and order | M3 Observe | chronology, scientific dating (cited) |
| HI.M.3 | Reconstruct and interpret | M6 Compare and reconstruct | source criticism, hermeneutics |
| HI.M.4 | Compare and quantify | M6, M5 Compute | comparative and quantitative history |
| HI.M.5 | Narrate and edit | M1 Represent | narrative, critical editions |

Examples. HI.M.1: dating a charter by its witness list and script, then testing its seal against the chancery's known forms. HI.M.2: converting an Old Style date to New Style before aligning it with a foreign dispatch. HI.M.3: reading a petition for what its author needed the reader to believe. HI.M.4: building a wage series from institutional accounts and stating how units were converted. HI.M.5: a critical edition that records every variant reading and the witness that carries it.

### Heuristics register (outside the count)
Read against the grain; ask who wrote it and why; beware hindsight; follow the money and the paper.

## 6. Face W — Warrants

| Cell | Primary | Second | Standard | Typical defeaters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| HI.O.A1 | W7 | W4 (material or forensic evidence) | two independent authenticated sources, or one with no plausible rival | forgery, single source, interpolation |
| HI.O.A2 | W7 | W4 (dating, owned by EA or PH) | date consistent across independent clocks | calendar confusion, reused material |
| HI.O.A3 | W7 | W9 + W6 | mechanism shown in the record; rival causes considered | post hoc reasoning, hindsight, teleology |
| HI.O.A4 | W7 | W6 + W2 | series built from records; method stated | survivorship of records, anachronism |
| HI.O.A5 | W7 | W8 | interpretation traced to the record | presentism, projection |

Examples. A1: a chronicle's report of a battle is accepted when an independent letter confirms it, and doubted when the chronicle copies an earlier source. A2: a deed dated by regnal year is rejected if the named witness died before that year. A3: a claim that a famine caused a revolt fails if the revolt began in a region with good harvests. A4: a rise in recorded crime is tested against a change in policing and record-keeping. A5: a commemorative speech is used as evidence of its own decade's beliefs, not of the event it praises.

W7 does not travel: a dated record fixes the occurrence, not a physical law.

## 8. Assignment rules
1. The dated fact is history's. That an event occurred, and when, is HI; every other pyramid cites it.
2. The model-explained path is the model's (EC, PO, ST); HI supplies the record.
3. A mathematician's life is HI; the theorem is MA; MSC 01 is the bridge.
4. A physical date (radiocarbon) is a measurement in EA or PH; its use for a human event is HI.
5. A strategic lesson drawn from a case is ST; the case's facts are HI.
6. A tradition-internal account of the past is IK or RE under W13; it enters HI.O.A1 only by W7.
7. A legal rule in force at a date is LA; the history of that rule is HI.
